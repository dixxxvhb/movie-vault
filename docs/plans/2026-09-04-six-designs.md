# The six designs, in full

Raw output of the 2026-09-04 design panel. Six independent designers, one
assigned angle each, then three judges on one rubric. The decision and the
synthesis live in `2026-09-04-no-vacancy-plan.md`; this is the source material,
kept because every one of these carries staging detail the plan compresses away.

Judges split three ways on the winner (Second Watch 119, Night Audit 117,
No Vacancy 116 aggregate). Read that as: they agree on the room and disagree
on the building.


---

## THE NIGHT CATALOGUE

**Pitch.** A private film collection has been deposited in an old building and nobody has written the catalogue yet. You are let in at night to do it. There are 47 rooms, each one a recreation of a scene, and none of them are labelled. You carry two things: a brass reader that shows you the one held second that made each room look the way it does, and a book with a page for every film and three blanks on it. Your job is to work out, from the room alone, where the collector filed each film, what he actually docked it for, and which other film in the building it is bound to. The answers are already true and already fixed. The book confirms three pages at a time and never tells you which one you got wrong. The building fills in behind you as you go, shelf by shelf, until the collection is standing up.

**Who the player is.** The night cataloguer. Not the collector, not a fan, not a ghost. Somebody hired to do an admin job on somebody else's stuff, who gets pulled in. This is the Obra Dinn framing and it is the single best framing available for rule 9, because it gives a total stranger a reason to care that does not require caring about Dixon: you have a task, the task is legible in ten seconds, and the person whose collection it is becomes interesting to you only because you keep finding his handwriting on the furniture. By the time you know who he is you are already invested. The KEEPER ribbon on the front desk flips the whole building to ink and no deduction, which is how Dixon himself uses it. Neither mode is the real one.

**Verdict on The Court:** reject

**Structure.** A converted five-level building, entered from the street in the rain.

EXTERIOR (30 seconds). A step, a wet awning, one lit window, an unlocked door somebody forgot. Uses the existing RainField system and atmosphere.jsx. Establishes closed, private, after hours.

THE ATRIUM (arrival, and the exit). Two storeys, dark, an unmanned front desk. On the desk: the Reader, the Catalogue, a handwritten note, and the VISITOR / KEEPER ribbon. The back wall carries 45 empty frames in a grid. That is the quote counter and it is visible in second one with no HUD. A panel by the desk states the only public facts: 47 rooms, average 8.55, one perfect ten, no name on the door.

GROUND, THE SEVEN WINGS. Not a corridor of 47 doors, which is a mall. Seven short galleries off the atrium, one per emotional_key: tense, dread, fun, cozy, awe, sad, camp. Five to nine doors each. The wings differ physically, not just in grade. Dread has a 2.2m ceiling and one working light. Camp is overlit and painted. Fun has carpet and a bench. Awe is double height. Every door carries a museum-style card on the outside. Score is nowhere in this organisation, which is the point.

MEZZANINE, THE HALL OF SHELVES. The filing spine, six stacked shelf galleries, the crown at the top: the crown (9.6 and up), the pay (9.0 to 9.5), the good night (8.0 to 8.9), the sevens (7.0 to 7.9), the shrug (6.0 to 6.9), the floor (below 6). Empty at the start. Horizontal spacing inside a gallery is the real score gap, so the walk from The Game at 6.8 to Annihilation at 7.4 is a genuinely long blank stretch.

TOP, THE STUDY. Twenty-one brass plaques, all blank. A law engraves itself only when you have confirmed the specific films it cites as evidence.

BASEMENT, CONSERVATION. Wet-lab light. The 34 Shoebox films as pencil rooms and the 30 Dark Drawer films behind doors that open onto a chair, a light, and a card.

THE UNBUILT WING. Off the basement, a construction corridor with an unfinished floor: 78 doorframes with no doors, each with a paper tag. The 78 pitched titles. Walkable from minute one, tags readable once 40 pages are confirmed.

**Why this shape.** A museum is organised by department, not by rank, and departments are the one taxonomy in this data that nothing currently uses: emotional_key is already authored on all 47 films and has never been rendered. It groups by feel, which is how a visitor actually decides which door to open, and critically it does not leak the answer. The score axis still exists, but it has been moved inside and turned into the reward: the Hall of Shelves is the thing you build, not the thing you walk in on. That is the Court's best idea kept and its worst consequence removed.

**Archive and pitched.** The archive is Conservation in the basement and it carries the game's one real quest. The 34 Shoebox films are pencil rooms, which the codebase already renders (fadedConfigFor and developedGradeFor are an invertible transform on a shared config, and the develop mechanic already exists). Certifying a pencil film runs that transform forward: the room develops, the card goes from pencil to ink, and the Catalogue admits it was scored from memory, not live. The 30 Dark Drawer films cannot be certified and the design says so out loud. Their doors open onto a chair, a light and a card that reads what he remembers, which is nothing. That is the truest room in the building and it costs four draw calls.

The 78 pitched titles get the Unbuilt Wing: doorframes with no doors and a hanging tag each. They are the rooms that were never built, which is literally what they are. Cheapest possible geometry for the largest unrendered content block: frames instanced to one draw call, tags to a second.

**Wayfinding.** Three layers, none of them a minimap. First, the wings are physically distinguishable by ceiling height, floor material and light count, so you know which wing you are in from anywhere in it without reading anything. Second, every door has a card and the cards are readable from three metres. Third, the player can leave pencil marks anywhere, which doubles as breadcrumbs. The atrium is always two turns away because every wing is a dead end that returns to it. There is no maze and no locked backtracking.

**Core loop.** Enter a room. It reads as a place after everyone has left. Walk it and find three to five indices, which are small physical wrongnesses: a second glass, a chair angled away from the table, a nail in a doorframe with nothing on it, a tape mark on the floor. Each index carries one clause of the hot take, verbatim, fused to the object it is about, so you read the take by walking. Raise the Reader at an index and the room drops to silent charcoal monochrome and one frozen second appears around you, staged with abstracted figures holding a pose. Walk around inside it. It collapses when you lower the Reader. Then open the Catalogue standing in the room and fill three blanks: the shelf, the charge, the kin. Leave. Do two more. The book locks three pages at once when three are simultaneously fully right, engraves them in ink, and puts three cards on the shelves upstairs. What changes: the Hall of Shelves physically builds itself, the taste laws in the Study engrave when you have proved the films they cite, and quote frames in the atrium fill in.

**Verbs.**

- **Walk** — WASD, stick, or touch pad. AABB slide collision, no jump, no crouch, no sprint. *(It is the only way to read a hot take, because the hot take is scattered across the room's objects. Movement is reading.)*
- **Look** — Pointer lock or drag. Free look, adjustable FOV, bob off by default. *(Indices are found by looking, not by a highlight sweep. The room rewards a slow pan the way a gallery does.)*
- **Read** — Hold right mouse, E, or the touch pad to raise the Reader. It snaps to the nearest index inside a 12 degree cone and 4 metres. Release to lower. *(This is the instrument and the entire second half of every room lives behind it. It requires no aim precision and no timing, which is deliberate.)*
- **Handle** — One context key. The game decides whether the object opens, spins, tilts, pulls or switches, using the six touch kinds already built. *(Collapsing six interaction verbs into one is both an accessibility win and a design win. The player never has to work out what an object wants. Turning a photo over to read the back of it should not require choosing a verb.)*
- **File** — Tab opens the Catalogue in your hands. Fill three blanks per page: shelf, charge, kin. Change any entry infinitely, no cost. *(It is the win condition, the progression, and the only scoreboard. It is also the only place the player asserts anything, which is what makes the confirmation click land.)*
- **Mark** — Leave a small pencil mark on any floor, wall or object. Persists in localStorage. One instanced mesh. *(Three jobs for almost no cost. A note to self, a breadcrumb for anyone who loses spatial orientation, and the one thing a visitor is allowed to add to a building otherwise entirely owned by somebody else.)*

**Signature mechanic: THE HELD SECOND** (build cost L)

Every room exists in two states. State one is the place after everyone has left: furniture, light, the mess. State two is reached by raising the Reader at an index. The room's colour collapses to charcoal monochrome over 0.35 seconds, all sound drops to one held synthesized tone with a slow beat frequency, the room's decorative detail layer unloads, and a frozen tableau of three to six abstracted figures appears, holding a pose, mid-action. You walk around inside it. Nothing moves. Nothing is explained. You lower the Reader and it is gone.

Build: a new `held` block per room in configs.js listing figures, positions and pose names. Eight named poses total across all 47 rooms (standing, turning-away, reaching, seated-forward, mid-sentence, looking-up, braced, fallen), each driving four joint angles on the existing abstractFigure capsule stack. Figures render as five InstancedMeshes, one per body part, so five draw calls regardless of figure count. The grade collapse pushes one entry onto the existing gradeBus with saturation to zero, contrast up, plus a cheap posterize in the existing post chain. The detail.jsx layer hides on entry, which recovers more draw calls than the tableau spends, so the held state is net cheaper than the lit state. Audio is one tone from the existing synthesis kit, no sample. Raycast for index snapping uses three-mesh-bvh, already installed as a drei transitive dep, zero new bundle.

*Why it is good.* It solves the exact problem the recon identified, that rooms are viewable and not explorable because there is no concealment, and it solves it without a bigger footprint. The concealment is modal instead of spatial. The same 4.2 metre box now holds two authored compositions and half its content only exists in the second one, so a room takes three minutes because there are genuinely two rooms in it.

It also lands every hard rule at once. Silent and monochrome means no sampled audio and no film footage. Abstracted figures holding a pose is exactly what rule 4 permits and nothing more. And it is the deduction instrument: the tableau is evidence, not illustration, so the pleasure is scholarship. Malignant's held second is a doctor, a chart and a lightbox. Sorry to Bother You's is a half open door you have to walk around the tableau to see behind. Same insane idea, and the difference between the two tableaux IS taste law two, felt rather than read.

**How a room gets richer.** Four levers, all inside 30 draw calls and 40k triangles.

1. The room is staged as a scene after the fact, not a diorama of the scene. Each room hides three to five indices: small physical wrongnesses you find by looking. A second glass. A chair angled away. A nail in a doorframe with nothing on it. Finding them is the exploration and it needs no new geometry vocabulary, just intent in the placement.

2. The hot take is fragmented onto the indices, verbatim, never assembled on a card. Memento's take reads "MY GOD WHAT THE FUCKKKKK BRO ... this has been my favorite thus far. it deserves the 5.0 and everything else should be lowered a percentage." In the Memento room, "MY GOD WHAT THE FUCKKKKK BRO" is on the Polaroid taped to the mirror. "it deserves the 5.0" is on the back of a photo you have to pick up and turn, which the existing spin touch kind already does. "and everything else should be lowered a percentage" runs along the inside edge of a dresser drawer and is only legible once the drawer is open, which the existing open kind already does. You cannot read the take standing still.

3. The Held Second doubles the room's authored content without doubling its footprint.

4. The room ends in a question, not an exit. Once you have found its indices, the Catalogue becomes fillable standing inside it, and you do the deduction in the room with the evidence around you rather than in a menu somewhere else.

On the 5.2 problem, which is the one the recon got wrong. Disclosure Day does not get an emptier room, because Dixon asked for more room, not for permission to build less. It gets a fully dressed first contact briefing room: long table, twelve seats, projection wall, the whole institutional apparatus, more built than most rooms in the building. What is barren is the EVENT, not the geometry. Raise the Reader and the held second is one figure, alone, mid-sentence, with a chart. Twelve chairs and one person talking. Its index is a projected diagram that grows larger over nine seconds while nothing happens, which is the bloodline note made physical: "Not a reveal, a zoom." You feel the 5.2 as a fully realised place where the attention drifted, not as a punishment box.

Per room draw call check: shell 4, ten to twelve props 12, indices batched to 2, info surfaces 3, held tableau 5. Worst case 26, and the tableau and the detail layer are mutually exclusive so live worst case is nearer 22.

**Progression.** Gated only by pages confirmed, never by grind and never by skill. All seven ground wings and all 47 rooms are open and enterable from minute one, including the Unbuilt Wing. Nothing about exploration is ever locked.

3 pages confirmed opens the mezzanine stair and builds your first shelf gallery.
12 opens the Study, and the 21 taste laws begin engraving.
24 opens Conservation, and with it certification of the 34 pencil films.
40 turns the lights on in the Unbuilt Wing and makes the 78 tags readable.

Confirmation is Obra Dinn's rule, adopted intact: a page locks only when three pages are simultaneously fully correct, and the book never tells you which of the three blanks on a wrong page was wrong. A wrong entry costs nothing but time. There is no fail state, no timer, no penalty, no hidden clock, and no way to lock yourself out. The only currency is pages, and the only thing pages buy is more building.

**The ending.** The front door of the atrium is unlocked from minute one and leaving is always allowed. When you leave, the building does the job you were hired for: it prints a catalogue. One page, in your hands, rendered.

It lists the rooms you actually stood in longest, in the order you stood in them. The shelf you filled first. The law you proved earliest. The door you walked up to twice and did not open. Then it hands over exactly one card from the 154 recommendations, chosen by which wing you dwelled in, not at random.

There is no completion percentage anywhere in the game, including here. A visitor who confirmed zero pages still gets a page, and it is not a scolding. Sample copy for that case: "You walked for eleven minutes and stood longest in the dread wing, in front of a door you did not open. Fine. Here is one to watch anyway." That line matters more than any of the others, because most strangers will be that visitor.

**Inclusivity by design.** Handled in the structure, not in a settings panel.

NO TWITCH, ANYWHERE. There is no timing, no reflex, no aim precision and no dexterity check in the entire game. The Reader snaps to the nearest index inside a generous cone. Every action in the world is: walk somewhere, look at something, choose from a list. This is a design decision, not a difficulty option, and it means the game is playable one handed, on a trackpad, on a phone, or slowly.

CONTENT WARNINGS ARE ARCHITECTURE. Every door in the building carries a museum card on the outside, so nobody has to open a menu to find out what is behind it, and reading the card is the normal thing you do at every door rather than a special accommodation. Most cards are dry. Hereditary's is not: "HEREDITARY. Grief, a family, and one thing in this room you will wish you had not looked at. It is dark and it does not let up. There is a bench outside this door for a reason." The card describes the ROOM, so it warns without spoiling the film.

YOU CAN DECLINE A ROOM AND KEEP EVERYTHING. Any Catalogue page can be filled from outside the room, using only the door card, the bloodline threads visible in the Hall of Shelves, and the film's neighbours. It is harder, and it is a real path. Nobody who does not want to enter Hereditary, Se7en, Sicario or Barbarian is locked out of the Study, the shelves, or the ending. This is the single most important line in the design.

COGNITIVE LOAD IS CAPPED BY DESIGN. One instrument. One book. Three blanks. Six verbs. Seven wings. No inventory, no resources, no currency, no map, no quest log. The whole system is explainable in one sentence and never grows.

READING IS CHUNKED. Nothing in the world is a paragraph. The longest text object in any room is one clause of a hot take. The written plot and verdict paragraphs live in the Catalogue, which you read seated, at your pace, with no timer. Sentence length in all game-written copy matches the source: short, plain, direct.

THE ROOM IS NAVIGABLE BY EAR. Every index emits a soft locating tone when you face near it, distinct in pitch per index. It is synthesized, so it costs nothing and breaks no rule, and it means a low vision player can find every index in a room by turning. It also makes the rooms better for everyone, which is the test.

THE STRANGER AND THE OWNER GET DIFFERENT DOORS INTO THE SAME BUILDING. The VISITOR ribbon is the deduction game. The KEEPER ribbon, sitting in plain sight on the front desk, fills every page in ink from the start and turns the building into a place to walk around and read his stuff. Dixon is a KEEPER. A stranger is a VISITOR. The game never calls KEEPER cheating, because it is his collection and he already did the work.

NO FAIL STATE. No timer, no penalty, no loss, no lockout, infinite retries, and the exit is unlocked from minute one.

**Three moments that sell it.**

- THE STING, AND THE NAIL. You are standing in the fake FBI office. It reads as an office: desks, a rail, a water cooler, men's coats. The index that finally catches you is a nail in the doorframe with nothing hanging on it. Raise the Reader and the held second is the room BEING BUILT: two figures carrying the desk in, a third holding a picture frame that has not gone up yet. You are not in an office. You are standing on a set that the characters built, and the film never told you either. You file the charge as taste law five, the antibodies tax is not automatic, and the Catalogue confirms it, because the bloodline note already says it out loud: "The FBI office isn't a lie the movie tells, it's a set the characters built."
- MALIGNANT INTO SORRY TO BOTHER YOU. Two rooms, two wings, ten minutes apart. In Malignant's room the held second is a doctor, a chart, and a diagram on a lightbox. Flat, explained, over. You cross the atrium into the fun wing and raise the Reader in Sorry to Bother You's room, and the held second is a door, half open, with something behind it you have to physically walk around the tableau to see. Same size of insane idea. One is a chart and one is a door. That is taste law two, delivered as a felt experience rather than a sentence, and the data says it verbatim: "One opened a door and made him look, one had a doctor explain a chart. 5.4 versus 9.4 is the whole doctrine." File both charges as law two and the plaque in the Study engraves itself while you are upstairs, and you hear it happen from the stairwell.
- THE DECREE. You have already filed The Matrix on the crown shelf and it is sitting there in the built gallery at 9.8. Later you confirm The Departed. You are standing in that same gallery when the shelf re-hangs itself: The Matrix's card slides one step down to make room. The link note is the whole beat, and it is real: "The tenth that moved. The Departed arrived at 9.9 and he ruled The Matrix down to 9.8 on the spot to make the room. First time a new film displaced a standing number by decree." A museum where the exhibit physically re-hangs itself while you are looking at it, because the collector changed his mind, is the moment a player describes to somebody else.

**What it sacrifices.** Pace. This is a slow game. Nobody runs, nobody jumps, nothing chases you. A player who heard "full game engine, feel like a video game in every sense" and pictured movement will find very little of it here. What they get instead is density, and if they do not want density they will bounce in four minutes.

Immediate legibility. The Court is easier to hold in your head than seven wings on five levels. A motel with numbered doors on floors is a diagram; my building is a building, and a visitor can get turned around in it. The wing differentiation, the door cards and the pencil marks are mitigations, not a cure.

The ranked wall as a first impression. Withholding the scores is the engine of the whole design, and it costs the site the single thing it currently does best, which is showing you a wall of 47 films in order in one glance. KEEPER mode buys most of that back, but a stranger who lands cold does not see the ranking until they earn it, and some of them will never earn it. That is a real trade and I would still make it, because the ranking as a first impression is a list, and a list is what a website already is.

Authoring cost on the existing 16. The bespoke rooms keep their geometry, lighting, colour grade and every QA lesson baked into their constants. But each one needs three to five indices placed, its hot take fragmented across them, and one held second staged. Roughly half a day each, sixteen times, plus one authoring pass assigning each of the 47 films one of the 21 laws as its charge, which is about two hours and several of which the data already states.

The 78 pitched titles get a corridor, not rooms. That is a lot of content given a deliberately thin treatment. I think the thin treatment is correct and moving, but it is thin.

No multiplayer, no leaderboard, no sharing, no photo mode.

**First thing to build.** One vertical slice, three rooms, two weeks, and it either produces the click or the design is dead.

Build MALIGNANT, SORRY TO BOTHER YOU and THE STING. That specific three, for four reasons: they are exactly one three-page confirmation lock, so the slice tests the whole progression rule; they share taste laws two and five as their charges, so the slice tests the Study engraving; they carry real bloodlines to each other and to The Game in the shipped links.json, so the kin blank has genuine answers; and two of the three are already bespoke rooms with hand-composed geometry and lighting, so two thirds of the level art exists.

Ship in the slice:
1. The Reader. Hold to raise, cone snap onto the nearest index, three-mesh-bvh raycast. No new deps.
2. The Held Second for those three rooms. Eight-pose abstractFigure extension, five InstancedMeshes, gradeBus collapse to monochrome plus posterize, detail.jsx layer unload, one held synthesized tone per room.
3. Indices with the hot take fragmented onto them, verbatim, in all three rooms. Reuse the existing spin and open touch kinds.
4. The Catalogue as a held 3D object with three blanks per page, backed by a vault-truth.json baked by emit_vault_data.py. Three-page simultaneous lock, no per-field feedback, infinite edits.
5. One Study plaque, law two, engraving on confirmation of Malignant and Sorry to Bother You.
6. The state machine swap underneath it: zustand replaces App.jsx's single string parsed by twelve startsWith checks. Already installed transitively, zero bundle cost, and nothing else in the design can be built on top of the current one.

The kill test is one specific feeling. Walk around Sorry to Bother You's held second, see the half open door, then open the Catalogue while standing in the room and file the charge as "an unguessable IDEA is not the same as an unguessable REVEAL." If that does not click, throw the design away and pick another one. Do not build the atrium, the wings, the shelves, the basement or the Unbuilt Wing until it clicks.

One prerequisite worth flagging on day one: emotional_key is authored on all 47 films upstream but emit_vault_data.py does not currently emit it into public/vault-data.json, and the seven wings depend on it. That is a one line change and it should go in before anything else, because the wing taxonomy is what makes the building non-spoiling.

## GHOST LIGHT

**Pitch.** The Vault becomes a small theatre, and every film he has scored is a number in tonight's show. You start in a seat in the house, watching a bare bulb on an empty stage. A set builds itself out of that light. You can stay in your seat and watch the number performed, or you can walk down the aisle, step up onto the stage, and call it yourself. Calling means this: hold one key and the stage shows you its marks, glowing spike-tape crosses on the floor. Walk to the lit one, release on the beat, and the room does a thing. Lights snap. Walls travel. The camera gets taken and handed back. The set you walk back into is not the set you left. Every room is a piece of staging with three to five beats, and the beats are built out of the film itself, so The Sting cons you, Malignant explains its own trick before you can see it, and Memento's marks are numbered backwards so the last beat you call is beat one. Nothing fails. Miss the beat and the room performs it slurred instead of clean, which is a different thing to look at, not a punishment. Between numbers you are in the lobby with the callboard, the house rules on the wall, and the coming-attractions cases full of everything he was offered and turned down. At the end of your program the sets strike, the ghost light comes back to center, and the house tells you what it noticed about you.

**Who the player is.** You are in the audience at somebody else's show. That is the whole framing and it does the heavy lifting for a stranger. You did not write these opinions, you are not being tested on them, and the house never once addresses you as if you did. It says "he" about the man whose taste this is, and "you" about the person in the seat. A stranger walking in cold understands the deal in four seconds because a theatre is the most legible building in the world: there is a stage, there are seats, there is a program. The second thing you learn is that you are allowed onstage, which is the transgression the whole game runs on. You are an audience member who gets to call the cues. That is a fantasy nobody gets in a real theatre and it is exactly what a choreographer's archive should feel like from the inside.

**Verdict on The Court:** reject

**Structure.** THE HOUSE. You open in a seat, third row, house left. Raked seating rendered as one InstancedMesh (60 seats, one draw call), a proscenium opening 6m wide, and an empty stage with a ghost light on a stand at center. This is the title screen and there is no other title screen. THE STAGE. Every film's set is built inside that proscenium at the existing room scale, roughly 4.2 x 4.2 x 2.6, and the crucial change is that the wall facing the house is GONE. The fourth wall is open. From inside any room you can always see the auditorium you came from, which doubles the perceived volume of every set for the cost of drawing the house once and sharing it, and it means you are never lost, because out is always the direction with the seats in it. THE LOBBY. Reached by walking up the aisle instead of down it. Carries the callboard (tonight's running order, plus all 47 numbers, plus the standby list), the house rules (the 21 taste laws as framed cards, most of them face-down until their evidence is visited), the frame wall (45 empty frames, one per quote, filled as you find them), and the coming-attractions cases holding all 78 PITCHED titles poster-out behind glass. Pitched films were offered and passed over, and a coming-attractions case is exactly the right object for a thing that was advertised and never played. BACKSTAGE. Behind every set, always, one turn away. One shared corridor kit reused for all 47: unpainted flats seen from the brace side, rope, worklights, and the cue sheet wall with 47 hooks on it. Backstage is the real map. Its doors are the bloodlines. THE MOTEL ROOM IS PRESERVED BYTE-IDENTICAL AND BECOMES THE SET OF MEMENTO. It does not become 'your room' and it does not stay a hub. Memento is a motel room, the room is already built to a standard nothing else touches, and a wall of 47 Polaroids hanging in a motel is diegetically perfect as the Memento note wall. The hub dissolves into the show, which is the most theatrical move available: the menu was a set the whole time.

**Why this shape.** The Court is a good building and it is the wrong building for this angle. Its vertical axis puts 9.9 upstairs and 5.2 in the basement, which quietly licenses building less for a low score, and the brief explicitly warns against that. A theatre solves the same problem better: a 5.2 gets the same stage, the same proscenium, the same lighting rig and the same three minutes as a 9.9, because that is what a running order is. Score stops being architecture and becomes POSITION IN THE PROGRAM, which is a choreographer's actual unit of judgement. He does not rank pieces by putting them on higher floors, he decides what goes where in the show. Second reason: a theatre gives the design a fourth wall, and once you have a fourth wall you have the entire vocabulary of reveal, misdirection, strike, and blackout, which is the whole angle. Third: the house gives a stranger a seat, and a seat is the most generous thing you can hand somebody who has just arrived in one man's memory. What I keep from The Court: the instinct that the existing motel must survive untouched, the refusal to put a number on a wall, and the idea that the gap between scores should be physically felt. Mine feels it in the running order and in the grease pencil on the callboard, not in metres of empty breezeway.

**Archive and pitched.** The 78 PITCHED titles are the coming-attractions cases in the lobby, poster forward, behind glass, lit from below. They were offered and passed over and a lobby case is what a building does with a film that was advertised and never played. Each case carries the real pitch reasoning on the card under the poster. The 34 Shoebox titles (scored from memory, in pencil) and the 30 Dark Drawer titles (seen, unscorable) are the STANDBY LIST on the callboard, and their sets exist and are walkable, but they are lit with worklights and half-dressed, taped out on the deck, and NO CUES ARE HUNG. You can walk a standby set and read what he remembers, verbatim. You cannot call it, because there is nothing to call, and the room tells you that in its own language rather than with a lock icon. Apollo 13's card says 'seen once or twice, not recently, a big favorite, confirmed 2026-08-13' and stands on an empty deck under two worklights, which is a more honest picture of a hazy memory than any UI could draw. You can drop a standby into the request box in the lobby, which puts it in your own next program as a rehearsal, not as a score. Nothing a visitor does ever touches his numbers.

**Wayfinding.** Three physical objects, zero HUD. One, the fourth wall: from anywhere onstage the auditorium is visible behind you and that is always the exit. Two, the callboard in the lobby: tonight's running order in order, numbers you have called struck through in grease pencil, house notes beside the ones that need them. It is a real theatre object doing its real job. Three, the ghost light: whenever the stage is empty it is on, and it is visible from the lobby, the house and the wings, so the building always has one lit point of reference. Backstage navigation is by the cue sheet wall, where an empty hook is a film you have not called and a full hook is one you have, so the counter is a gap in a wall of paper and there is no percentage anywhere in the game.

**Core loop.** Sit, watch a set build. Walk up onto it. Hold CALL and the floor shows you four marks, one of them warm and pulsing, the rest cold. Cross the stage to it, feel the pulse in the light before you hear it, release on the pulse. The beat fires: a light snaps, the counter becomes a betting cage, three figures turn to face you. The set is now different, so the props you already read have moved and the hot take fragments printed on them are in new places, so you walk the room again and it is not the same walk. The next mark warms somewhere else, usually across the room, so travelling the set is the pacing. Three to five beats and the number closes, the flats strike, and you are in the wings with a cue sheet in your hand and a door in front of you that leads to whatever film shares a bloodline with the one you just called. What changes: the set, permanently for the length of the number, plus your cue sheet wall backstage, plus which taste laws have crystallised in the lobby, plus what the house picks for your next program.

**Verbs.**

- **MOVE** — Walk and look. WASD plus mouse on desktop, stick plus drag on touch, unchanged from what ships today. *(Crossing the stage between marks IS the pacing of a number. The beats are placed so that calling all of them means walking the whole set, which is how a 4.2m box gets three minutes out of a player without a single locked door.)*
- **CALL** — Hold to reveal the set's marks as glowing spike-tape crosses on the deck, with the next beat's mark pulsing warm and the rest cold. While held, the room breathes down: practicals dim, the grade drops a notch of saturation, the audio recipe pulls back to its pulse. Release while standing on a mark to fire that beat. Release anywhere else and nothing happens and nothing is lost. *(This is the game. One key that turns a room into a performance and a player into the person calling it. It is legible in one second, it is his own working vocabulary, and it makes walking the room mandatory without making anything mandatory.)*
- **SIT** — From any seat in the house, or from the single chair that exists at the back of every set, hand the number to the house and watch it performed at full quality, hands off. Press anything to stand back up and take it over mid-number. *(It is the inclusivity of this design and it is also the fantasy. Every number is watchable by somebody with one input, a bad connection, three minutes, or a motor disability, and it costs nothing to build because the house-performed version is the same cue stack with an autopilot walking it. It also makes the game a thing you can leave running on a second monitor, which is how an archive like this actually gets shown to people.)*
- **HANDLE** — The existing touch layer. Nudge, open, press, spin, swing, switch. One key, contextual, on the room's small objects. *(It already works, it is already authored across 29 interactions, and in this design it earns a new job: the hot take is fragmented onto handleable props, so the only way to read what he actually said is to touch the room. Sixteen of those 29 are currently 'nudge', which stops being a shrug once nudging a prop is what uncovers the next four words of 'holy fucking shit that was a terrible fucking movie hahaha'.)*

**Signature mechanic: CALL THE BEAT** (build cost M)

Every set carries a CUE STACK: an ordered list of three to five beats, each anchored to a MARK, which is a position on the deck rendered as a glowing tape cross plus a faint standing figure showing you where you would be. Hold CALL and the room breathes down and shows every mark, the next one warm and pulsing on the tempo of that room's existing audio recipe, the rest cold. Walk to it, release, the beat fires. Beats are built from a fixed vocabulary of seven parameterised systems so that 47 numbers is a config table rather than 47 builds: STRIKE (props travel to new authored transforms on a timeline, zero new draw calls, pure lerp on meshes already in the scene), SNAP (instant light and grade change through the existing lightRig and gradeBus), FLY (the room takes the camera for two to six seconds along an authored path and hands it back, using CameraRig's existing 780ms eased flight), TURN (the shell group rotates around the player), DOUBLE (figures spawn or copy, using the existing Duplicates system and abstractFigure prop), STRIP (material swap to blocking and braces on the same geometry, showing the flats are flats), and REVEAL (occluders travel, using the existing CurtainReveal geometry now cued instead of looping). The timing half: release on the pulse and the beat hits CLEAN, meaning the snap is hard, the strike is fast and lands on the accent, the audio gets its transient. Release off the pulse and the beat hits SLURRED, meaning the same beat runs 40 percent slower with a soft ease, the light comes up rather than snapping, and the room keeps a slightly muddier grade for the rest of the number. Both are finished, authored, good-looking states. There is no fail, no score, no timing meter and no combo.

*Why it is good.* It converts the single biggest measured weakness into the single biggest strength. Today every system in the codebase runs on a wall clock: CurtainReveal opens every 45 seconds whether or not a human is in the room. That is counts without a cue, and it is exactly why a room reads as a diorama. Attaching every effect to a mark and a release turns the same code into staging. It is also the one mechanic that a choreographer of 100 plus competition pieces will recognise instantly as his own job, because hitting your mark on the count and having the lights find you is literally the thing. And the clean-versus-slurred split encodes his taste system without a scoreboard: same steps, done on the music or not, and only one of them is dancing.

**How a room gets richer.** A set is not a room, it is a room over time, and that is the whole answer to how ten primitives fill three minutes. A four-beat number is four rooms of dressing for the price of one, because a STRIKE is a transform lerp on meshes that are already resident. Take The Sting, 9.6, oldest film on the wall. One kit: a table, a chairRow, a counter, a barShelf, a paperScatter, three abstractFigures, a lightDisc, a door, a mirrorPlane. Beat one stages it as a back room, low and warm, the figures seated. Beat two is a FLY that takes your camera to the window across the room, and while you are looking away the STRIKE runs behind you: the counter becomes a betting cage, the chairs form a row facing it, the figures stand. You get the camera back at the window, you turn around, and it is not the room you left. Beat three brings the whole thing to full house lights, which is when you notice the mirror was showing you the wrong room the entire time. Beat four STRIPS it: materials swap to unpainted flats and braces, the ceiling opens to the grid, and it was a build. His actual words, on the deck in tape: "i KNEW the FBI place was a fucking build as well but just was under the wrong assumption haha". Nine primitives, four rooms, one draw-call budget. Three more multipliers on top. First, the fourth wall: the auditorium sits behind every set and is drawn once and shared, so a 4.2m box always has a 20m view out of it. Second, the hot take is fragmented across the handleable props, and the props MOVE between beats, so the text relocates and rereading the room after a strike is a genuinely different walk. Third, backstage: every set has a real back you can walk into, where the flats are braced and labelled and the next number is waiting in the dark, and that costs one shared corridor kit for all 47. Concealment, which the recon correctly identified as entirely absent today, arrives for free the moment a set has beats, because beat one is allowed to hide what beat three shows you.

**Progression.** The unit of progression is the PROGRAM, a running order of five numbers, and it is never a gate. Every one of the 47 films is on the callboard from the first second and walkable at any time. What the program gives you is a reason for the next five, which is what a stranger actually lacks. Program one is fixed and hand-authored to teach the game by contrast, not by score: Memento (learn the verb in the room that is already built), Baby Driver (learn that timing changes the beat), The Sting (get conned by a beat), Malignant (watch a room explain its trick before you can see it), Sorry to Bother You (get floored by the same idea staged correctly). That order is an argument and the argument is one of his own taste laws, danced. From program two on, the house assembles the next five out of what you actually did. Three things accumulate, all of them by visiting rather than by winning. First, CUE SHEETS: call a number's beats in order and its sheet goes on the hook backstage, which also unlocks that room's alternate staging. Forty-seven hooks, and the empty ones are the only counter in the game. Second, HOUSE RULES: the 21 taste laws hang face-down in the lobby and turn face-up when you have visited the specific films cited as their evidence, so the antibodies law turns over after you have called both The Game and The Sting, and the reveal-as-event law turns over after Malignant and Sorry to Bother You. A face-up law adds its cited films to the callboard as a bracket, so an argument you uncovered becomes a route. Third, DOUBLE BILLS, which open when you have called both halves and are then permanently joined by a door. Nothing here can be ground and nothing here can be failed. Sitting through a number counts exactly the same as calling it.

**The ending.** The last number of a program strikes and does not get replaced. The house lights come to half, the flats go out, and the ghost light rolls to center on a bare deck. Then the curtain call, run backwards: the four or five sets you spent the most time in each re-form for three seconds and perform their single most characteristic beat, in the order you saw them, then go dark. No stats, no percentage, no completion. When the last one goes, one paragraph of type appears on the deck in front of the ghost light, in the register of the source, assembled from the house rules that actually turned face-up for you and the dwell the building measured. Something like: "You sat down twice and stood up once. You spent nine minutes in The Sting and forty seconds in Hereditary, which is roughly what he did. He said anxiety is not excitement and he is not doing the homework, and you appear to agree with him. Here is one he has not watched yet." Then one card, from the 154 recommendations, with his real reasoning printed on it, handed to you as an object you can pick up and turn over. Then nothing. The ghost light stays on, the house is still there behind you, and you can walk back into it and call anything on the board, or leave. A theatre that never goes fully dark is the correct last image for an archive of one person's nights.

**Inclusivity by design.** Seven design decisions, none of them a menu toggle. One, SIT is a first-class verb, not a fallback. Any number can be watched performed by the house at full quality from a seat, with one input, on any device, at any point, and standing up mid-number takes it back. The game is complete for a player who never walks onstage once. Two, no-fail is structural. Off-tempo produces the slurred beat, which is an authored, good-looking, fully finished alternate version, not a retry. Nothing anywhere in the game is gated on timing, reflex, or accuracy. Three, content warnings live in the fiction as house notes on the callboard, in theatre language, because a callboard is where a real stage manager posts exactly this: "HEREDITARY. Strobe in beat three. Sudden loudness at the ceiling. Sit this one and you will still get it." The existing flashPolicy governs the beat, and the board tells the truth about which beat it touches, before you commit to walking in. Four, leaving costs nothing ever. Backstage is one turn away from every set, the fourth wall is open behind you, a half-called number stays half-called on the board with no penalty and no reset, and no progress is ever stored inside a room. Five, sound is never load-bearing. The tempo pulse is visible in the mark's glow before it is audible, and every audio cue has a lighting cue riding the same event. Six, the pronoun. The house says "he" about Dixon and "you" about the visitor, always, and never once implies the visitor holds these opinions. That single choice is what makes 47 rooms of one man's profane opinions read as generous to a stranger instead of as a quiz. Seven, the reading is optional and layered. The only instruction in the game is a lit cross on the floor. The hot takes are verbatim and profane and stay that way, but they are decoration you choose to walk toward, never the thing you must parse to proceed. Total verb count is four and there is no inventory, no jump, and no crouch.

**Three moments that sell it.**

- THE STING, beat two. The room takes your camera and flies it to the window across the back room, holds you there for four seconds, and gives it back. You turn around and the counter is a betting cage and every figure is standing. You did not see it happen because it happened behind you, which is the entire film. Then beat four strips the flats to braces and the tape on the deck reads: i KNEW the FBI place was a fucking build.
- THE DOUBLE BILL. Malignant, 5.4, is a room that over-explains: every beat you call brings a worklight up on a diagram that tells you the trick before you can look at it, and you physically cannot call that room late because it has always already told you. Its last beat opens a door straight onto Sorry to Bother You, 9.4, mid-number, where a beat is a door you have to walk through yourself and what is behind it turns to look at you all at once and then multiplies to the horizon. Four points apart, same idea, staged two ways, back to back, and his own bloodline note is on the shared wall: one opened a door and made him look, one had a doctor explain a chart.
- MEMENTO. The set is the motel you already know, unchanged, and the marks on its floor are numbered backwards. Every beat you call un-does the room: the notes on the wall un-write, the drawer un-opens, the light climbs back toward an earlier hour. The last mark you are able to call is beat one, and calling it shows you the thing that started all of it, and then the whole set resets clean and you understand you were never going to see it in order. Ten out of ten, the only perfect score on the board, and the only number in the show whose cue stack runs the wrong way.

**What it sacrifices.** Four honest costs. First and biggest, free-form exploration. This design says a room has an author and a running order, which means a set is on rails in a way an open archive is not. A player who wants to poke at everything in any sequence gets less here than they would in a hub-and-spokes museum, and the mitigation, alternate stagings unlocked by cue sheets, is a partial one. Second, authoring load. Forty-seven numbers is 47 pieces of choreography, and a lazily authored number is worse than no number at all, so this design will look bad in the middle of its build in a way the current diorama approach never does. The seven-system vocabulary is what makes it survivable but somebody still has to make 47 sequences that hit. Third, completeness. The 78 pitched titles stay posters behind glass and the 30 Dark Drawer titles stay names on a standby list under worklights, and both are deliberately less than a room. That is defensible and it is still less content than a design that gave every one of the 223 titles a space. Fourth, the hub goes away. Turning the motel into the Memento set is the move I believe in most and it is also the one that costs something real: the person who liked walking into a motel room and seeing the whole ledger wall at once has lost that as a home base and now has to go up the aisle to the callboard for the same information.

**First thing to build.** One number, with the house behind it, and it must be BABY DRIVER, 8.4, whose entire hot take is "The genius is in the verbs, not the nouns. Big fan." Build the house geometry (raked InstancedMesh seating, proscenium, ghost light on a stand), the open fourth wall, and the SIT verb. Build the cue-stack runner and exactly two beat systems, STRIKE and SNAP. Build the mark: tape cross, cold and warm states, the pulse driven off the existing baby-driver audio recipe. Then author four beats. The room starts still. Each beat you call on the pulse locks one element of the room into the count, a wiper, a gearshift, a signal light, a door, and each beat you call off the pulse leaves that element visibly rattling out of phase for the rest of the number, until beat four either resolves the whole room onto one count or leaves it as a room where three of four things are almost together. Nothing else. No lobby, no backstage, no bloodlines, no second film. This is the right slice because Baby Driver tests the hardest and most falsifiable half of the design, the claim that clean versus slurred is a difference a player can feel and wants to chase, in the one room where getting it wrong would be most obvious. If holding a key, seeing four crosses, crossing the stage to the warm one, and releasing on the pulse does not produce a snap that feels good in the hand, the entire design is dead and you will know inside a week for the cost of two systems and a room you were going to build anyway.

## THE ASSAY

**Pitch.** A man scored 47 films to one decimal place and left the numbers off the doors. You are handed a loupe and a building full of rooms. Walk a room, look at what is in it, and put a number on the door. Then the real number appears next to yours, permanently, and if you were wrong the basement wakes up and hands you the rule you did not know. Twenty-one rules, all of them real, all of them written by him, all of them earned by being wrong in exactly the shape the rule describes. Collect enough evidence for a rule and it stops being text on a brass rod and becomes part of your loupe: it starts marking rooms for you. The endgame is not seeing every room. The endgame is walking into a room you have never seen, raising the loupe, and knowing the number before you set the plate. You are not touring a man's taste. You are learning to run it.

**Who the player is.** You are the person who came to price the place. Not a fan, not a friend, not an archivist. An appraiser with a loupe and an empty book, standing in a motel court at night where every door has a room behind it and a blank brass plate on it. The framing does zero work explaining who Dixon is and it does not need to, because you meet him the way an appraiser meets anyone: through what he valued and by exactly how much. A stranger with no film knowledge is fully equipped on arrival, because the evidence is in the rooms, not in the movies. You are never asked "did you like this film." You are asked "what did this room cost him," and the room tells you if you walk it.

**Verdict on The Court:** adopt-with-changes

**Structure.** A six-storey exterior-walkway motel in a U around a night courtyard, floor = score band, horizontal position = real score, so the spacing on each walkway is the actual gap between his numbers.

The measured floors, from the data: GROUND holds two rooms, Disclosure Day 5.2 and Malignant 5.4, sitting alone at the bottom of a mostly empty deck. FLOOR ONE holds three, Hereditary 6.7, Under the Silver Lake 6.7, The Game 6.8, and between it and the ground floor is a full storey of nothing, because he has no 6.0 through 6.6. FLOOR TWO holds ten, from Oblivion, Sunshine and Annihilation triple-parked at 7.4 up to Triangle, Obsession and Source Code at 7.9. FLOOR THREE holds ten, Moon 8.1 to Coherence 8.9. FLOOR FOUR holds nine, Stardust 9.0 to Batman Begins 9.5. FLOOR FIVE holds thirteen, and it is absurd, and it should look absurd, because his average is 8.55 and the building has to be visibly top-heavy for that to be a fact you feel instead of a fact you read. On floor five there is a bay where five doors touch with no gap at all: The Dark Knight Rises, Enemy, The Sting, Se7en, L.A. Confidential, all 9.6. There is no gap between them because there is no gap between them.

The ANNEX is the older wing across the courtyard, unlit, 34 rooms, the memory-scored Shoebox. Its plates are pencil and smudged and the existing develop mechanic stays, with a new job: developing an archive room is how you get its rough number at all.

The DARK DRAWER is the 30 seen-but-unscorable titles, and they are not rooms. They are bricked arches along the ground-floor colonnade with a title scratched into the mortar. Loupe a bricked arch and you get the seen_note, which is usually flat and often devastating: "hazy, seen long ago."

The OFFICE is the night manager's office, ground floor, and it is where every visitor starts. The loupe is on the counter. Behind the counter is the key rack.

The PLANT ROOM is one flight below the courtyard: a rack of 21 vertical brass rods, one per taste law, thickness set by the law's own weight so the weight-5 rods (antibodies, execution meritocracy, idea versus reveal) are visibly the load-bearing ones and the rack leans toward them. Unearned rods are down and dark. Earned rods stand and hum, with the law etched on the flat face at reading height. Evidence cards hang off each rod, one per citing film, blank until you have been in that room.

**Why this shape.** The Court is right and I am keeping it, but I am changing what it discloses. A building whose vertical axis is the score axis tells you the score from the street, and if the entire pleasure is calling the number before you see it, that geometry spoils the game forty-seven times.

So: the floors are not labelled and the plates start blank. The building gives you the neighbourhood and hides the tenth. That is exactly the right information split, because Dixon's system runs on tenths. He said "sicario beats those. bump sicario to 9.9 it needs it's own number for sure." The tenths are the doctrine. Thirty of the forty-seven films sit between 7.4 and 9.9, which means the interesting discrimination is entirely inside a band the architecture cannot leak. You know the top-floor room is high. You do not know if it is 9.3 or 10.0, and that is a real question with a real answer that a room can argue.

This is the Outer Wilds contract. You get the whole solar system on day one. The mystery was never where things are.

**Archive and pitched.** The 78 PITCHED titles are the best asset nobody has rendered and they get the endgame. The office key rack has 78 hooks, one film each, offered and passed over. Take a key and the machine will BUILD that film a room from its metadata, genre, year and bloodline relations, using the existing six-family template engine with zero new bespoke work. Then you call it. And there is no answer key, because he never watched it. Your number is the only number that will ever be on that door. That is 78 doors in a building otherwise entirely one man's, where the visitor's opinion is the record, and it is the cleanest possible answer to the requirement that a stranger be given a reason to care.

There is also a SECOND, SMALLER RACK, four keys, no law rods attached: Masters of the Universe 8.5, Stardust 9.0, Rogue One 8.2, Catch Me If You Can 9.3. The machine cannot price these. They are the affection picks and the doctrine does not cover them. The game says so out loud and does not explain them. Every rule system needs the thing outside the rules or the rules read as a cage.

The ARCHIVE and its develop mechanic stay exactly as built and become the Annex above.

**Wayfinding.** The courtyard is the map and there is no other one. Standing in it you see every door on every floor. Called plates are lit. Uncalled plates are dark. Floor height gives you the band. Horizontal gap gives you the distance between two of his opinions, so the walk past nothing between 5.4 and 6.7 is the hole in his board and you feel it in your legs.

The courtyard light level rises one notch per rod fired, twenty-one notches, so the sky over the Court goes from full dark toward dawn as you learn. That is the progress bar and it has no number on it.

Bloodline doors are the shortcut network, 39 of them, each labelled with its real relation on the frame. Circulation between floors is by RAMP, not stairs, both because it is gentler for motion-sensitive players and because a ramped building is a legible building. The only stair is the single flight to the roof, and there is a service lift shaft beside it that goes to the same place.

**Core loop.** Cross the courtyard, pick a dark plate, step through a threshold into a room. Walk it, including the pocket you cannot see from the door. Raise the loupe: line items appear pinned to the props that earned or cost them, verbatim in his words, plus or minus. Read the room's argument. Step back out to the plate, dial a number, commit. His number engraves itself beside yours and both stay there forever. If you were close, a bloodline door opens somewhere in that room and now leads to a linked film. If you were wrong, a rod fires in the basement and you have a new law. Go down and read it. See which films are cited as its evidence. Go visit one of those. Come back up smarter. What changes: your loupe. Every law you complete makes it mark more, until it is marking the things you would have missed and you are reading a room the way he does.

**Verbs.**

- **Walk** — WASD, stick, or touch stick. Two speeds set by ground, not by a button: 2.6 m/s on the breezeways and in the courtyard, 1.4 m/s once you cross a room threshold. *(The horizontal spacing of the Court is the score gap made real, so crossing the 60-foot dead stretch between Malignant at 5.4 and Hereditary at 6.7 has to be walkable without punishing anyone. Speeding up outdoors and slowing down inside is the level telling you where thinking happens. No sprint key, ever.)*
- **Look** — Pointer lock, touch drag, existing controller. *(Half the rooms argue their score through sightlines. Malignant shows you the answer from the doorway and Sicario refuses to show you the whole room from anywhere. Looking is the primary evidence-gathering act and it needs no verb dressing.)*
- **Loupe** — Hold right mouse, Shift, or two fingers. A film loupe rises. Inside the circle: line items pinned to the props that earned or cost them, verbatim from his hot take, marked plus or minus. Also every fired law you hold, listed on the inner bezel. It marks the nearest twelve items in a cone, never a pixel raycast. *(This is the entire UI. There is no HUD, no bottom row of DOM buttons, no info card floating in the air. It replaces the three fixed self-lit planes the game has today, which is a net reduction in draw calls. It is also how the hot take gets fragmented onto props so that walking is the only way to read it, and it is the object that visibly gets smarter as you learn, which is the whole angle in one held button.)*
- **Touch** — E, or tap. Existing Touchable layer. A prop responds once and means something. *(It stays, but the rule changes: 16 of 29 current interactions are nudge, and nudge is now banned as a default. Every touchable in a room must change a state that is part of the room's argument. If it does not, it is decor and it does not get a prompt.)*
- **Set** — E on a plate opens a dial: two digits, one decimal, thumbstick or scroll or drag. Commit, or decline. E on a law rod in the basement reads the law and its evidence cards. *(It is the only commitment the game asks for and it is fully reversible forever. Declining a plate engraves his number immediately and marks the door uncalled: you get the content, you skip the game. That is the honest inclusive option and it costs a button, not a settings screen.)*

**Signature mechanic: The Plate** (build cost L)

Every door in the Court carries an empty brass number plate. You walk the room, then set your number. Your number scratches itself into the brass in a hand. His number engraves beside it, deep and clean and permanent. Both stay forever and both are visible from anywhere in the courtyard. Missing does not print an error. It fires a rod in the basement: the specific taste law whose shape your error matched. Call Annihilation high on visuals and the thin-ensemble law fires. Call Disclosure Day generously because it is expensively made and the reveal-as-event law fires. Call The Sting low because it is a 1973 twist movie and the antibodies law fires with its exact caveat: the tax only applies when the pleasure lives in the surprise. The plate is revisable forever. Pull it, re-dial, no penalty, no cost, no counter.

*Why it is good.* It converts a private opinion archive into a rule system you can operate, and it makes being wrong the primary acquisition mechanic instead of a fail state. It is also the only design here where the reward for understanding is a better tool rather than more content: a fired law changes what your loupe can see. And it turns the single most game-shaped fact in the whole dataset into a physical event. The Departed arrived at 9.9 and he ruled The Matrix down to 9.8 on the spot to make room. Set your plate on The Departed and the plate on The Matrix, visible from that same walkway, physically slides a tenth. You watch a number move because a new number needed the space.

**How a room gets richer.** A room today is one 4.2 x 4.2 x 2.6 box that shows you everything from the doorway, built from 21 primitives, where 16 of 29 interactions are nudge. Five rules fix that, and all five fit the budget.

ONE, NO ROOM IS ONE BOX. Every room is a threshold vestibule of about three metres, plus the box, plus at least one pocket you cannot see from the door. Occlusion is a wall and a wall is twelve triangles. This is the cheapest change with the largest effect and the existing shellParams system already supports it.

TWO, THE BEST LINE ITEM LIVES IN THE POCKET. The prop carrying the strongest fragment of the hot take is always the one hidden from the door. Coherence's pocket holds the second house. From inside it you can see a third down a sightline, and past that a fourth. "it wasn't just 2 houses. or 3. its fucking infinite bitch" becomes the payoff of walking rather than the caption on a plane.

THREE, ONE PROP PER ROOM IS A MECHANISM. Not decor. A required slot in the config schema. Memento's mirror. Predestination's loop. Enemy's second figure, always exactly where you are not looking. The mechanism's state change IS the room's argument about the number.

FOUR, EVERY ROOM HAS A BACK. One findable position from which the room composes into a single shot. Stand there and the grade snaps to the film's designed five-colour palette at full saturation and holds while you stand. Nothing unlocks. It is just correct, and it teaches you the room was composed by someone. Cost: one distance check per frame against a 0.8m disc.

FIVE, ONE SYSTEM PER ROOM, CHOSEN TO ARGUE THE SCORE. The systems library already has sixteen behaviours. LookAwayGrow in Barbarian at 7.6: the thing grows when you look away, which is dread that works. LookAwayGrow in Malignant at 5.4: the thing grows in front of you, unmissable, from the first second, which is his complaint made spatial. Same primitive, opposite verdict.

AND THE PRINCIPLE UNDER ALL OF IT: SCORE IS NEVER SIZE, SCORE IS COHERENCE. I reject the proposal that a 5.2 room is barren, because he asked for more room and because it is bad design to punish the visitor for his bad night. Disclosure Day gets the biggest room in the building, because his reaction was enormous. "it was all speech for like the last hour." So it is a vast lecture hall, lectern that never stops, seating going back two hundred metres, and the alien silhouette is visible from the doorway for the entire walk toward the reveal curtain at the far end. Nobody built less. Somebody built the complaint at full scale.

Every room gets the same 30 draw calls, the same 40k triangles, the same lightRig layers. The difference is agreement. In a 9.9 room every light has a source you can walk over and touch, one palette runs the whole space, and no position shows you everything. In a 5.2 room the light comes from nowhere locatable, the film's five-colour palette is split into two halves that fight, and every sightline shows you the ending. Zero extra cost, entirely legible to someone who has never seen the film, and it is the actual argument he is making.

Budget arithmetic: threshold 200 tris and 1 draw, occluder walls 100 tris on a shared material and 0 new draws, mechanism 1 to 3 draws, loupe marks 1 instanced draw and only while held, composed-shot disc 0 draws. We delete the three self-lit info planes and their three textures. Net close to break-even inside 30.

**Progression.** Nothing is gated by skill and nothing is gated by grind. All 47 rooms are open from second one. You can walk into Memento in the first thirty seconds and you should be allowed to.

LAWS unlock two ways, so both the guesser and the completionist get there. One: you set a plate and miss in a law-shaped way. Call Annihilation an 8.9 because the shimmer wall is gorgeous, plate reads 7.4, and the thin-ensemble law fires. Two: you visit both ends of a bloodline. Walk Malignant and then Sorry to Bother You in any order and the reveal-as-event law fires on its own, because that pair IS the evidence and links.json already says so.

EVIDENCE CARDS fill by coverage. Each of the 21 rods in the basement carries one blank card per film that cites it. Visiting the film fills the card. The gap in the rack is the counter and there is no percentage anywhere.

ROD FIRING happens when a rod's cards are all filled. A fired rod is not a checkmark. It becomes a live filter in your loupe. Fire the thin-ensemble rod and from then on the loupe circles ensembles it judges thin in every uncalled room. Fire the antibodies rod and the loupe starts flagging whether a room's pleasure is located in its surprise or in its craft. You are literally building your instrument out of what you understood.

BLOODLINE DOORS open when you have stood in both rooms. 39 of them. They are the fast travel network and each one carries its relation name on the frame: antibodies, villeneuve, loop-bracket, moon2, displaced by decree.

THE ROOF opens after five plates. Five, not forty-seven.

**The ending.** The stair past Room 10.0 goes up one flight to a flat roof with a folding chair, a card table, and one last blank plate. The plate is not a film. It is your visit. Dial a number on yourself.

Then the table engraves its own, computed from your route: where you dwelled, which rods you fired, which rooms you called before you raised the loupe at all, which door you went back to a second time. It does not score you. It describes you, in his register, and it is allowed to be rude.

"Fine, your turn. You spent eleven minutes in Enemy and forty seconds in Maverick. You called The Sting a 7 and then you went straight back to it. You are a slow-burn guy who does not trust fun, which is a diagnosis, not an insult."

Then one card, face down on the table. One of the 154 recommendations, picked by which rods you fired and which rooms you held still in, with his actual reasoning on it. Not a list. One film. If your route showed you left a tension room and came back later, the card carries a second line: "Do not split this one across two nights." That line is his law, applied to you, and it is the moment the game reveals it was reading you the entire time.

You leave with a permalink to your own plate wall: 47 doors, your numbers and his, side by side, engraved.

**Inclusivity by design.** NO-FAIL IS STRUCTURAL, NOT A MODE. Being wrong is the acquisition mechanic. There is no score for your scores, no accuracy percentage, no streak. A miss by 3.0 fires a bigger law than a miss by 0.3. The plate is revisable forever with no cost and no counter. There is nothing in the game you can lose.

THE PLATE IS SKIPPABLE WITHOUT LOSING THE ROOM. Set-and-decline engraves his number immediately and marks the door uncalled. You get every word of the content, you just do not play the guessing game. Someone who wants to walk a beautiful building and read a man's opinions can do exactly that, start to finish, and never dial a number.

READING LOAD IS CHUNKED BY GEOMETRY. The longest single text object in the game is one taste law, around forty words, etched on a rod you walk up to. Hot takes are fragmented into three to eight line items of five to twenty words each, pinned to props. There is no paragraph anywhere. This is a large accessibility improvement over the current three info planes and it is the same move as the core design, which is the test of a good one.

CONTENT WARNINGS ARE A DOOR, NOT A POPUP. Hereditary, Se7en, Barbarian, The Substance and their neighbours get a three-metre vestibule between the breezeway and the room, with the warning etched at eye height and a second door you have to open yourself. You can stand in the vestibule, read it, and turn around, and the game counts that door as visited. The evidence card fills. Nothing is lost. That is opting out of a room without losing progress, made literal in the architecture rather than promised in a menu.

THE STRANGER AND THE OWNER GET DIFFERENT FRONT DOORS WITHOUT A PROMPT. Everyone spawns in the night manager's office, because that is where the loupe is and where the thirty-word note is. Room 4 is his room, it is unlocked, and it is simply the fourth door you pass. Nobody is asked to identify themselves.

MOTION. All floor-to-floor circulation is by ramp, which is both gentler for motion-sensitive players and the reason the building reads as accessible rather than as a set. One stair to the roof, with a lift shaft beside it going to the same place. No sprint, no jump, no crouch, no head bob by default.

NO TIMER ANYWHERE, EVER. The one thing Outer Wilds does that I am explicitly refusing is the clock.

THE LOUPE NEVER REQUIRES AIM. It marks the nearest twelve items in a cone, never a pixel raycast, so touch and gamepad and mouse all get identical information density.

EVERY AUDIO CUE HAS A VISUAL TWIN. A rod firing is a sound, and the rod standing up, and the courtyard sky stepping one notch lighter. Nothing is audio-only. The 41 synth recipes stay exactly as they are, as atmosphere, never as information.

TWENTY MINUTES IS A COMPLETE VISIT. The roof opens at five plates. A twenty-minute route reaches the ending, gets its recommendation card, and gets its permalink. Mastery takes three hours. The short version must not feel like a demo and that constraint is a hard rule on every content decision, not a nice-to-have.

PROFANITY IS VERBATIM AND ANNOUNCED. The office note says so in his own register. That is the honest version of a content filter and it respects both the visitor and the source.

**Three moments that sell it.**

- THE MALIGNANT INTO SORRY TO BOTHER YOU PAIR. You walk Malignant first. It is a big, expensive, well-lit room and the answer is standing there visible from the doorway, and past it a corridor of six doors that all lead back to the same place. You call it a 7, because it is obviously competently made. The plate reads 5.4 and a rod fires: an unguessable IDEA is not the same as an unguessable REVEAL. Then you walk Sorry to Bother You. Its room is a corridor with one door in it, and the door OPENS while you are looking at it, and what is on the other side is staged in front of you and never explained. You call it a 9. Plate reads 9.4. Same rod, second citation, and it lights. From this moment your loupe marks every reveal in every uncalled room as staged or explained. You were not told the rule. You were hit by it twice, from both sides, and it turned into a tool in your hand. That is the entire game in eight minutes and it is why this is the vertical slice.
- THE STING'S BLOODLINE DOOR ONTO THE GAME. You have called The Sting at 9.6 and The Game at 6.8 and a door appears in The Sting's back wall with one word on the frame: antibodies. Walk through it. Two con movies, twenty-four years apart, same genre, same twist architecture, a 2.8 gap. And etched on the inside of the doorframe is his actual reasoning, which is that the tax only applies when the pleasure lives in the surprise itself. You stand in one room and then the other and you can feel where the pleasure is located in each. It is a museum wing that only exists because you earned both ends of it, and it explains a 2.8-point gap without a single word of essay.
- THE DEPARTED MOVING THE MATRIX. You set your plate on The Departed on floor five. His engraves at 9.9. And then, twenty metres down the same walkway and fully in your sightline, the plate on The Matrix physically slides a tenth, from 9.9 to 9.8, and settles. Because that is what happened. He ruled The Matrix down on the spot to make room for a film that arrived the same night, and links.json calls the relation displaced by decree. You watch a number move because a new number needed the space, in a building where position is meaning. Nobody has ever built that moment because nobody else has a dataset where a man moved a score by decree and wrote down that he did it.

**What it sacrifices.** AWE ON FIRST CONTACT. A spectacle design opens on a view. This opens on a night manager's office, a loupe on a counter, and a note asking you to think. The first sixty seconds are the quietest of the five proposals on the table and I am choosing that deliberately, because the payoff is a curve and curves start low. If the panel wants a screenshot that sells the project in one frame, this is not the design that produces it. The courtyard at dawn with twenty-one rods fired is that frame, and you have to earn it.

CASUAL BROWSABILITY. The plate is friction, on purpose, and some fraction of visitors will bounce off being asked to commit to a number. Set-and-decline mitigates it, but a person who declines every plate is walking a very pretty museum and is not playing this game, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

BREADTH OF ROOM SUBJECT. Because every room now has to argue a number, the room's brief narrows. A room can no longer just be a beautiful evocation of a film. Sunshine's room has to make the case for 7.4 with its own geometry, which means the thin ensemble has to be a legible spatial fact and not merely a mood. That is a real constraint on the room authors and it will occasionally fight the more purely atmospheric instinct that produced the best of the existing sixteen.

AUTHORING COST IN A PLACE THAT IS NOT VISIBLE. The law-diagnosis map, which error shape on which film fires which of the 21 laws, is 47 rooms times a handful of plausible error directions each. It is a couple of hundred authored mappings and none of it is a mesh, a texture, or a screenshot. It is the most expensive invisible thing in the design and if it is done lazily the whole mechanic reads as random.

AND THE HONEST ONE: REVERENCE. This design argues that a man's taste is a system with rules, and part of what makes taste beautiful is that it is not. The doctrine genuinely does not cover Masters of the Universe at 8.5 or Stardust at 9.0, and rather than force them into the machine I have put four keys on a second rack that the machine cannot price and let the game admit it. That is the best version of the concession, but it is still a concession, and someone could reasonably say that turning a person into a predictable instrument is a colder read of him than he deserves.

**First thing to build.** THREE DOORS, ONE LAW, ONE LOUPE. Not the Court. Not the App.jsx state-machine rewrite. Not the engine.

Build a single short breezeway with three doors: Malignant 5.4, Sorry to Bother You 9.4, The Sting 9.6. Two of those rooms already exist as bespoke components (Stby.jsx, Sting.jsx), so the room work is one new room, Malignant, built to the five richness rules. Add the Plate on all three doors, the Loupe, and exactly one rod in a small plant room: an unguessable IDEA is not the same as an unguessable REVEAL. All three films cite it, which is why these three and not any other three.

That is roughly a week of build against the existing codebase and it tests the only thing that can kill this design.

THE TEST: put someone in front of it who has seen none of the three films. They walk the three rooms, set three plates, read one law. If they come out of it saying some version of "oh, he pays for the staging, not for the idea" then the thesis holds, the rest is content and geometry, and you can commit to the Court with confidence. If they come out saying "I had no way of knowing that," the diagnostic layer does not communicate, the design is dead, and it died for one room instead of forty-seven.

Ship it as its own route on the existing static build so it can go live and get real strangers on it before a single wall of the Court is modelled.

## THE SECOND WATCH

**Pitch.** A motel strip at night. Ten doors are lit. Behind each one is a place from a film, and the first time you walk in there is no commentary, no score, no opinion, nothing of Dixon at all. It is just the room, and you are in it cold, exactly the way he was. Somewhere in that room there is one thing you are seeing wrong from where you are standing. When you go stand somewhere else and it resolves, the room changes: the light shifts, the props you ignored turn out to have his handwriting on them, a door you had not noticed becomes a door. That is the whole game. Walk in, be wrong about something, go stand somewhere else, get the man's voice as a reward instead of a toll. On the way out of every room there are two doors and each one is painted with something he actually said about that movie, and you leave by the one that is closer to what you felt. Nobody is scoring you. At the end of the night you go back to your own room, the door closes, and the Vault tells you what kind of watcher you are based on where you and he disagreed, and gives you one thing to go watch.

**Who the player is.** You are staying at the motel. That is the entire framing and it is deliberately thin, because the strongest version of this is not roleplay. You have a room, Room 4, with your bed and your wall of Polaroids, and it is night, and the strip outside has other rooms with the lights on, and nobody is going to stop you from looking. The archive belongs to a guy called Dixon who is not here. You find that out gradually, from his handwriting, the way you find out about a person from their apartment. A stranger does not need to be told who he is in order to walk into a room and be surprised by it. By the time his voice shows up they have already had the experience he is talking about, which is the only order in which one person's opinion of a film is interesting to somebody else.

**Verdict on The Court:** reject

**Structure.** THE STRIP. Flat, single storey, one straight motel exterior you can walk end to end in about forty seconds. No stairs, no verticality, no ranking anywhere in the geography. Your room, Room 4, is untouched: the bed, the north wall of Polaroids, the archive, all of it exactly as shipped. It is now a room you live in rather than the universe. You leave through the actual door onto a concrete walkway under a sodium lamp with a parking lot on the other side.

There are 47 doors on the strip and exactly TEN are lit on any given night. The ten are drawn the way his slate actually gets drawn: one dread room, one from the fun shelf, one mind-bender, one under 7.0 on purpose, one from the Hazy Wing, and five spread across the rest. The other thirty-seven are dark doors with brass numbers. Ten doors is a choice a person can make. Forty-seven doors is a spreadsheet with a floor plan.

At the far end of the strip, past the last room, is the ICE MACHINE ROOM. That is the archive: the 34 Shoebox titles in pencil with their memory scores, developable exactly as the existing develop mechanic already works, and the 30 Dark Drawer titles that have no number and cannot be developed, with a sign that says so in his voice. This is the one interior on the strip that is not a film.

The PARKING LOT holds the 78 PITCHED. Twelve cars a night, instanced, each one a film that got offered and passed over, with its pitch reason on the dash where you can read it through the glass. One action per visit: you can push one car under the sodium lamp. That promotes it to a lit door on your next visit. It is the only thing you do that lasts, it is one decision, and it is exactly what a pitch is.

Room interiors stay 4.2 x 4.2 x 2.6 or whatever each bespoke room already is. Nothing about the existing sixteen changes structurally. Budget: the whole exterior is about twelve draw calls. One instanced mesh for 47 door slabs, one canvas atlas for the numerals, one for the walkway and rail, one instanced body plus one glass pass for the cars, and the sodium lamp is a baked falloff texture on the ground rather than a real light. Residency stays inside the max-3 rule: your room, the strip, and the one film room you are inside. Exactly three.

**Why this shape.** The Court is a leaderboard with stairs. Score is already fully expressed by the north wall in your own room, and rebuilding that same diagram at architectural scale spends the entire scale-up budget on navigation, which is the least interesting problem in the project and the one that was already solved. Worse, it commits two design crimes. First, it puts Memento on the top floor and Disclosure Day in the basement, so everybody rides to the top, and the funniest content in the whole dataset ("it was all speech for like the last hour", "voldemort did it first and was way more chill about it") becomes the part nobody walks to. Second, and this is the fatal one against hard rule 9: a ranked building tells a stranger "here is one guy's favourite movies in order", and a ranking is only interesting if you already trust the ranker. A stranger does not. You cannot open with the number.

The strip is flat because his archive is flat. Disclosure Day at 5.2 and Memento at 10.0 were watched by the same guy on the same couch about six weeks apart. Putting them next door to each other is the truthful geography. Nothing is a summit and nothing is a basement, so the low scores get visited, and the low scores are where the jokes are.

Ten lit doors instead of forty-seven also buys, for free, the one thing a 47-room game desperately needs and has no other way to get: a reason to come back. A second visit is a different ten. Zero new content, real return value.

**Archive and pitched.** The archive is the ice machine room at the end of the strip, and it also leaks into the nightly ten: one of the ten lit doors is ALWAYS a Hazy Wing door. That room is built WRONG on purpose. Proportions slightly off, one prop in it that does not belong, a doorway a bit too narrow. Because he is remembering it, not watching it. His own law says the bad memory is an asset and long-gap rewatches play like first watches, so a hazy room is a first watch with the furniture in the wrong place, and if you develop it later in the ice machine room the geometry corrects itself. That is the single best unbuilt idea sitting in this data and it costs one extra transform on configs that already exist.

The 78 pitched are cars in the lot. Not rooms, not doors, not a menu. They are the movies that did not get in, parked outside, and you can look in the windows and read why they were offered. Pushing one under the lamp is the player's only lasting act.

**Wayfinding.** You can always see the whole strip from anywhere on it. There is no map, no minimap, no compass, no HUD. Your own room is the only door with a light on inside and a Polaroid taped to it, so you can never lose your way home. Doors you have already been through tonight go dark, which is the only progress indicator in the game and it is a light switch, not a counter.

The escape hatch for anybody who came here wanting one specific film: your Polaroid wall is still the index. Take a Polaroid off the wall in your room and that film's door lights up on the strip whether it was in tonight's ten or not. The curated ten is a suggestion, never a cage.

**Core loop.** Step out of your room onto the walkway. Ten lit doors. Each door has a plate on it with the date he watched it, where he watched it, whether it was a rewatch, and one warning line in his voice if the room needs one. You pick a door on that alone.

Inside: the room, cold. No score anywhere, no title card, no floating text, no Dixon. The room has its own weather and its own noise and it is doing something whether or not you are there. You walk around. Somewhere in that room is one object or one sightline that reads one way from the doorway and a completely different way from exactly one other position on the floor. You have to physically go stand somewhere else. When the sightline resolves, the room FLIPS: the light rig changes, the grade shifts, and every prop you already walked past now carries a fragment of his verbatim hot take, readable only up close. The plot and the verdict paragraphs, if you want them, are on one object you can pick up. You are never made to read a paragraph to proceed.

Then you leave. Every room has two doors out and each is painted, in the film's own palette, with a real sentence he said about that film. In Malignant: "voldemort did it first" on one, "it kinda hands you the problem" on the other. Neither is correct. You walk through whichever is closer to what you felt. If the film has a bloodline, the door on the reveal side leads directly into the linked film instead of back to the strip, and you chain rooms without ever touching the walkway.

What changes: the door you came out of goes dark. The Vault has quietly logged which line you picked, where you walked, and how long you stayed. That is the only state it keeps and it is never shown to you as a number.

**Verbs.**

- **Walk** — WASD, touch stick, or hold a direction. The existing AABB slide collision and the existing controller, rebuilt off walkKeys.js's hardcoded four-key object into a rebindable intent map. *(It is the entire game. The signature mechanic is literally 'go stand somewhere else', so walking is not traversal between the content, walking IS the content. Every reveal in all 47 rooms is solved by moving your feet, never by aiming, timing, or clicking.)*
- **Look** — Mouse, pointer lock, touch drag, or arrow keys. Free look with an FOV control that lives in the pause menu but is not the accessibility answer. *(Half the reveals are occlusion and parallax. You have to look past a thing, behind a thing, or along a thing. Look is what makes standing somewhere else pay off.)*
- **Touch** — One context action on the nearest interactable, reusing the shipped Touchable and TOUCH_KINDS layer. Critically: every touch target ALSO fires on a two second proximity dwell, so it can be triggered by walking near it and waiting. *(Sixteen of the twenty-nine interactions in the shipped game are 'nudge' and that is fine. Touch is not where the depth is. It is the small physical acknowledgement that you are a body in the room, and the dwell fallback means nobody is ever locked out of content by their aim.)*
- **Leave through a line** — Two doors out of every room, each painted with a different real sentence he said about that film. You walk through one. No confirmation, no prompt, no UI. *(This is the whole ending mechanic disguised as a door. It gathers the divergence data that makes the exit meaningful, it never asks the player a question, it never scores them, and it puts his verbatim voice on the single object every player is guaranteed to look at. It also solves the reveal-side bloodline door with no extra vocabulary.)*
- **Take** — Two objects in the world can be lifted and moved: a Polaroid off the wall in your own room (which lights that film's door on the strip), and one car in the parking lot, which you push under the sodium lamp to promote it into a future night. *(It is the only verb that changes the world past this session, and it is deliberately limited to two uses so it never becomes inventory management. One is the escape hatch from the curated ten. One is the player's single act of curation, which is the exact thing this archive is about.)*

**Signature mechanic: Go stand somewhere else** (build cost L)

Every room contains exactly one thing you are wrong about from where you enter, and the correction is purely positional. There is no puzzle, no combination, no click target. An object or a sightline reads one way from the doorway and resolves into something else from one other spot on the floor, by occlusion, parallax, anamorphosis, or simply being able to see the back of something. Standing in that spot with a clear line trips the room's REVEAL.

The reveal flips the room into its second state. Same geometry, same draw calls, zero new meshes: a second grade and a second light preset applied over the identical props, plus a visibility mask that turns on the fragment textures. This is the archive's develop transform generalised. fadedConfigFor and developedGradeFor already prove that an invertible transform over a shared config is the right shape for this codebase, and the second watch is the same trick pointed at every room instead of just the pencil ones.

In the second state, and only then, his verbatim hot take is fragmented across props you already walked past. Not a floating card. Magnets on a fridge, pencil on the back of a flat, a note in a mirror. Walking around is the only way to read it.

Worked examples on real films, all buildable from the existing prop vocabulary:

ENEMY 9.6. Two apartments, mirrored, reading as two rooms from the door. Walk to the far corner and the mirrorPlane resolves. It was one room the whole time. Which is his own debrief verdict: one man, and Adam is the alibi.

THE STING 9.6. The FBI office. There is a two foot gap behind the back wall. Slip behind it and it is plywood, bracing and sandbags with the lit side glowing through the seams. Nothing explains it. His bloodline note already says it: the FBI office is not a lie the movie tells, it is a set the characters built.

COHERENCE 8.9. The window is the only bright thing in the dining room. Go to it. The house across the street has the same window with someone at it, and behind that one another, instanced, and it does not stop.

SE7EN 9.6. A box. You can walk all the way around it and there is no angle that shows you. The reveal is that there is no reveal, and that costs exactly one box.

MALIGNANT 5.4. A figure with a chart explaining, continuously, whether or not you are listening. Go stand behind the chart. It is blank on the back. There is nothing there. It is just a chart.

MEMENTO 10.0. The note wall reads as a system from the door. Read it reflected in the mirror over the sink and the order is different, and the order is the lie.

*Why it is good.* It is the cheapest possible mechanic and it is also the exact thesis of this man's taste. His single highest-weight law is that an unguessable idea is not the same as an unguessable reveal, that a wild concept delivered as exposition does not land, and that it has to be staged as an event happening in real time. A reveal you walk into is an event happening in real time. A reveal on a floating info card is exposition. The mechanic IS the doctrine, which means every room in the game is quietly arguing his position just by working.

It also solves the measured problem the recon actually found. Rooms show you everything from the doorway and that is precisely why they are viewable and not explorable. This puts one thing in every room that the doorway cannot see, so the doorway stops being sufficient, so you walk.

And it fixes the stranger problem. The first watch has no owner in it. A stranger gets a place and a surprise. His voice arrives second, as the payoff, about a thing they just experienced. That is the only sequence in which a stranger cares about a stranger's opinion.

**How a room gets richer.** A 4.2 x 4.2 x 2.6 box of ten primitives becomes worth three minutes when it has these five things, and every one of them already has a shipped implementation in this repo.

1. ONE SIGHTLINE THAT ONLY RESOLVES FROM ONE SPOT. Cost: zero new draw calls. One authored floor position, one AABB trigger volume using the collision math already in colliders.js, and one raycast to confirm the line is clear. three-mesh-bvh is already present as a drei transitive dependency at 62KB, so the raycast is free. This is the reason to be looking, and without a reason to be looking no amount of dressing makes a room explorable.

2. A SECOND STATE OF THE SAME GEOMETRY. Cost: zero new draw calls, zero new triangles. A second grade object and a second lightRig preset over identical meshes, plus a visibility mask. Precedent is already shipped and working in fadedConfigFor and developedGradeFor. This alone doubles the content of all 47 rooms without doubling the geometry budget.

3. THREE TO FIVE PROPS CARRYING THE HOT TAKE IN FRAGMENTS, readable only up close and only after the flip. Cost: one 1024x1024 canvas atlas per room, comfortably under the 8MB texture budget, generated by the same canvas technique used everywhere in this project already. Coherence gets "its fucking infinite bitch" one word per fridge magnet. Ex Machina gets "GURL ARE YOU AVA?!" scratched into the glass of the cell. Bullet Train gets "might watch Thomas episodes tonight" on a train timetable. This is what makes walking around the only way to read him.

4. ONE AMBIENT SYSTEM THAT LOOPS WITH NO REGARD FOR YOU. Cost: already built. DustDrift, RainField, PulseBeat, StreakLights, SwarmEvent, GlyphRain, PeripheralFigure, ScheduledCut are all shipped. Set its tempo from the film's real runtime so a 169 minute film breathes slower than a 99 minute one.

5. TWO DOORS OUT, EACH PAINTED WITH A REAL LINE. Cost: two textured slabs.

And the thing that stops all of this collapsing into "low score means less room": SCORE IS NOT CONSTRUCTION QUALITY, SCORE IS HOW MUCH THE ROOM NOTICES YOU. Every room is fully built, fully lit, fully dressed, at every score. What changes is attention.

9.5 and up, the room is a scene partner. It responds to where you look, it withholds, it flips hard, it waits for you. Memento, Sicario, The Departed, Blade Runner 2049, The Dark Knight, The Matrix, One Battle After Another.

8.0 to 9.4, the room performs on cue but does not watch you back.

7.0 to 7.9, the room is on a loop that ignores you entirely. Triangle at 7.9 is literally a loop. Source Code at 7.9 is literally a loop. Coherence's neighbours. This tier is nearly free.

Under 7.0, the room TALKS OVER YOU. It is expensive-looking, confidently staged, and completely uninterested in whether you exist. The Game at 6.8 runs its operatives around you on a script. Hereditary at 6.7 proceeds with the ritual and does not acknowledge you, which is exactly his complaint, that it is homework he is not doing. Malignant at 5.4 explains the chart forever. Disclosure Day at 5.2 gives the speech.

That gradient is cheap, funny, honest, and it means the bottom of the wall is somewhere people go on purpose.

**Progression.** There is no unlock tree, no XP, no completion percentage, and no skill gate. Progression is measured in NIGHTS.

A night is: ten lit doors, one of which is always a Hazy Wing room and one of which is always scored under 7.0. The night ends when you go back into Room 4 and close the door. That triggers the exit reading. Your next visit draws a fresh ten.

Three things carry across nights, and that is the complete list.

1. A door you walked stays walked. Its Polaroid on your north wall is now turned around, with the line you left by written on the back next to the line he actually used. The wall slowly becomes a record of the two of you disagreeing. That is the closest thing to a collection and it lives on a wall you already own, not in a menu.

2. The car you pushed under the lamp is a lit door tomorrow. One per night. That is the player promoting a film from the pitched pile into the ledger, which is the exact motion the real ritual runs.

3. Any Hazy Wing room you developed in the ice machine room stops being built wrong. Its geometry corrects, permanently.

Nothing is gated behind visit counts. If you want Memento on night one, take its Polaroid off your wall and its door lights up. If you want a different ten immediately rather than waiting for a real calendar day, the vending machine at the end of the walkway gives you one, and it says so plainly. Making a stranger wait a day for content is not scarcity, it is a way to lose them.

The taste laws are NOT progression and I am rejecting that proposal outright. Twenty-one paragraphs of analytical prose gated behind visiting the films they cite is a wiki with a fetch quest attached, and it puts the least-Dixon text in the game (the archivist's voice, not his) at the centre of the reward loop. The laws instead live where they belong: as the vocabulary the exit reading speaks in, and as one optional object in the ice machine room, a stack of index cards you can read if you want the thesis.

**The ending.** You walk back into Room 4 and close the door. The strip noise cuts. The room is quiet for a moment.

Your north wall has changed. Every Polaroid for a film you went into tonight is turned face-in, and on its back, in the film's own two colours, is the line you left by and the line you did not. Then the room says one thing, once, out loud and on the mirror, and it is built from where you DIVERGED from him rather than where you agreed. Divergence is the interesting signal. Agreement is flattery.

Example of the actual copy, generated from real route and real doctrine:

"You went into the dread lane three times tonight and did not touch the fun shelf. He does that too, and it is why the fun shelf sat understocked for a year until The Nice Guys proved counterprogramming works on him. You stood in Malignant for four minutes. He gave it a 5.4 and said it hands you the problem. You left by the other door. One of you is going to be right about Bugonia and it is on the queue. Go find out."

Then you get exactly one recommendation from the 154, with its real reasoning, chosen off that divergence. Not a list. One.

No percentage, no badge, no score, no "you have seen 12 of 47". The counter does not exist anywhere in this design. What you leave with is a sentence about yourself that you did not write and a single film to go watch, which is the only souvenir a film archive has any business handing out.

**Inclusivity by design.** CONTENT WARNINGS ARE ARCHITECTURE, NOT A MODAL. Every door has a plate, and for a dread room the plate carries a line in his own voice before you touch the handle. On Hereditary: "He gave this a 6.7 and said anxiety is not excitement and he is not doing the homework. There is a body in here and there is a ritual. There is no jump scare." Then you turn around or you do not, and turning around costs nothing because nothing commits until you cross the threshold. That is how a friend warns you, and it is the same surface that carries the date, the platform, and whether it was a rewatch, so it never reads as a special-needs annex bolted onto the front of the scary rooms.

LEAVING NEVER LOSES ANYTHING. A room counts as visited the instant you walk in. Not on completion, not on finding the reveal. You can walk in, feel wrong about it, and walk straight back out, and the night keeps your visit, the Polaroid still turns around, the exit reading still counts it. There is no partial state to lose because there is no completion state to reach.

NOTHING IN THIS GAME REQUIRES AIM, TIMING, OR REFLEX. The reveal is positional, so it is solved by walking. Every touch target also fires on a two second proximity dwell, using the DwellConcede system that already ships. There is no quick time event, no timer, no enemy, no failure state anywhere in the design. If you have not found a room's reveal after ninety seconds, it fires on its own, as though you found it, with nothing on screen saying you were helped. That last detail matters. Assistance that announces itself is not assistance.

THE STRANGER GETS THE SCENE FIRST AND THE OWNER SECOND. First watch has zero Dixon in it. This is the single most important inclusion decision in the design, because the failure mode of a personal archive is demanding that a stranger care about a person they have never met before they have been given anything. Here they get a place, and a surprise, and then a funny profane sentence from a guy who felt the same thing. It also gives Dixon, who knows all 47 takes by heart, the one thing he cannot otherwise have: his own rooms with the commentary hidden until he earns it back.

READING LOAD IS CAPPED BY DESIGN. Required text in any room is under thirty words, and it is always his spoken voice, which is short, plain and profane. The long plot and verdict paragraphs live on one optional object per room that you have to physically pick up. The twenty-one analytical taste laws are index cards in the ice machine room. Nobody is ever forced to read a paragraph to move forward.

COGNITIVE LOAD IS CAPPED BY DESIGN. Ten doors, not forty-seven. Five verbs. No HUD, no inventory, no map, no compass, no numbers on screen, no menu you have to learn. The only progress indicator in the entire game is a light switching off over a door you already walked through.

THE FLIP IS A CROSS-FADE, NEVER A STROBE. flashPolicy.js already governs this and it governs the second watch too. Rooms that want a strobe get motion instead. Baby Driver's room gets rhythm in the movement of objects, not in the light.

AND THE 5.2 IS NOT PUNISHED. Disclosure Day gets the biggest room on the strip. Building less for the films he liked less would be reading his ledger as a quality report, and it is not, it is a record of nights. Some nights were funnier than others.

**Three moments that sell it.**

- THE STING, 9.6, walking behind the FBI office. You are in a bright, busy government office being told a thing with total authority. There is a two foot gap at the back wall that you only notice because you went to look at something else. You slip behind it and you are standing in plywood, bracing and sandbags, with the lit side of the world glowing through the seams. Nothing tells you anything. There is no card, no chime, no voice. Then the room flips and on the back of one of the flats, in pencil, is the only thing you get: "it really was just simply happening in front of me." His bloodline note for this film literally already says the FBI office is not a lie the movie tells, it is a set the characters built. The level design just does what the note says.
- COHERENCE, 8.9, the window. The dining room is dim and the window is the only bright thing, so you go to it, because that is what people do. Across the street is the same house with the same window, and there is someone standing at it, and it is you. And behind that window there is another one, and behind that another, instanced out to the fog, and it does not stop. Then the fridge behind you flips and the magnets have been rearranged into one word each: its fucking infinite bitch.
- DISCLOSURE DAY, 5.2, walking out mid-speech. The worst film on the wall gets the biggest room in the game: a packed auditorium, a figure at a podium delivering the reveal speech, and it does not stop and it does not care that you came in. You walk the entire length of the hall, past rows of people who do not turn around, and from the back row you can finally see that the alien is standing at the side of the stage in plain sight, lit, waiting to be revealed, and it has been there since you walked in. You leave by the fire exit. Out on the walkway you can still hear the speech going, muffled, through the wall. His actual words are on the exit door: "it was all speech for like the last hour."

**What it sacrifices.** THE RANKING STOPS BEING A SPATIAL FACT. This is the real cost and the panel should weigh it honestly. The north wall in Room 4 still holds the score axis as a diagram, but the world no longer argues the ordering, and you can no longer walk the shape of his taste from 5.2 up to 10.0 and feel the gaps in your legs. That is a genuinely evocative thing The Court would deliver and I am choosing to lose it, because I think the ordering is simultaneously the least interesting fact in this dataset and the most alienating one to a stranger, and because the flat strip buys back accessibility, build cost, and a reason to visit twice.

EVERY ROOM NEEDS AUTHORED CONTENT TWICE. Forty-seven reveals and forty-seven second states. The transform architecture keeps the geometry cost at zero and the thirty-one template rooms can take a generic config-driven flip, but the sixteen bespoke rooms each need a hand-found reveal position and hand-placed fragments, and that is real hours by a person with taste. There is no version of this where a script writes those.

NO BROWSING BY DEFAULT. Ten doors a night means the film you came for might not be lit. The Polaroid-off-the-wall escape hatch fixes it completely, but it is one extra step and some visitors will bounce before they find it. The vending machine mitigates further. I accept the friction because ten legible choices beats forty-seven illegible ones.

NO COMPARISON TO OTHER VISITORS. Static hosting, no server, no database at runtime. The exit reading tells you what kind of watcher you are and it can never tell you that eleven other people picked the same door. That would be excellent and it is not on the table, so I am not going to pretend otherwise.

THE 78 PITCHED GET LOOKED AT, NOT ENTERED. They are cars in a lot with their reasons on the dash. That is honest to what they are and it is cheap, but it is not a room and anybody hoping the pitched pile becomes explorable space will be disappointed. Only the one you promote ever becomes a place.

AND: A PLAYER WHO WANTS A GAME WITH FAILURE WILL NOT FIND ONE HERE. There is nothing to lose, nothing to beat, and no way to get it wrong. That is deliberate and it is the inclusion answer, but it means the design has no tension of its own. All the tension is borrowed from the films.

**First thing to build.** ONE PAIR OF ROOMS, BOTH STATES, WITH THE BLOODLINE DOOR BETWEEN THEM: MALIGNANT 5.4 AND SORRY TO BOTHER YOU 9.4.

Not the strip. Not the exterior. Not the state machine rewrite. Build the pair that IS the doctrine, because the shipped bloodline note for these two already reads: "Comparably insane ideas. One opened a door and made him look, one had a doctor explain a chart. 5.4 versus 9.4 is the whole doctrine."

That one slice tests every load-bearing claim in this design simultaneously.

It tests that a 5.4 can be a genuinely good room, because Malignant's room is the figure and the chart, fully built, fully lit, explaining forever, ignoring you, and the reveal is walking behind the chart and finding it blank.

It tests score-as-attention, because you have the bottom of the gradient and the top of the gradient side by side and you can feel whether "the room talks over you" versus "the room is a scene partner" actually reads as a difference a player notices.

It tests go-stand-somewhere-else in both its cheapest form (walk behind a flat surface) and its purest form, because Sorry to Bother You's reveal is a door in an ordinary corridor that you open, which is what the man's own note says the difference was.

It tests the flip, because both rooms need a second state and a fragmented hot take, and both hot takes are gifts: "nahhhh it didn't really work for me. it kinda hands you the pro..." and "YOURE FUCKING KIDDING", the latter of which he audibly yelled at an empty apartment.

It tests the two-doors-out mechanic on a case where his real sentences genuinely split, and it tests the bloodline door as a physical exit rather than a map.

And it tests the build economics honestly, because Stby.jsx already exists as a 585 line bespoke room, so you are measuring the cost of retrofitting a second state onto a finished bespoke room, while Malignant has no bespoke component at all, so you are simultaneously measuring the cost of building a new one to this standard. Those are the two numbers that determine whether 47 rooms is a plan or a fantasy.

Ship it behind a query flag on the existing motel so nothing else has to move yet. If walking from Malignant's chart through that door into Sorry to Bother You is not the best ninety seconds this project has ever produced, the design is dead and it cost you one room.

## THE NIGHT AUDIT

**Pitch.** A motel on a highway, 2am, and you are the new night auditor. Your shift is midnight to dawn. The owner is not here. He never is on your shift, because he checks in, watches a movie, writes one line on the pad by the phone, and goes to bed around 2. Your whole job is the job every night auditor has: walk the property, look in the rooms, close the book before the sun comes up. There are 47 rooms on the walkway and each one is a film he watched, in the order he watched it, so walking left to right is walking July 13 to August 31 of one summer. Every room still has the movie running in it. Every room also has a light switch by the door that turns the movie off and the motel back on, and half of what he left behind only shows up in one of those two lights. Nothing chases you, nothing can be failed, nothing can be missed forever. At dawn you go into the office, read what the register wrote about you while you walked, take the one card the clerk left on the desk, and ring the bell.

**Who the player is.** The night auditor, first shift. This framing does four things no other framing does. It gives a stranger a legitimate reason to be in a stranger's rooms, which is the difference between snooping and a job. It explains the owner's absence without a single sad note attached to it: he is not gone, he is asleep, he goes to bed around 2 and the Vault runs from midnight. It licenses the one activity the whole game is made of, because reading the register IS the job. And it hands the design a natural clock and a natural ending, the shift and the dawn, without inventing a fantasy for either. You are staff. You have keys to everything. Nobody is watching you. That is the most permission a game can give somebody, and it costs nothing to grant.

**Verdict on The Court:** adopt-with-changes

**Structure.** One motel, one storey, wrapped in a U around an asphalt lot. Not two to five storeys. Doors run along an exterior walkway under a flat roof on posts, and the run is CHRONOLOGICAL, not score-ranked. Left to right is July 13 to August 31, 2026, the real watch dates in the ledger. There are 35 nights and 47 films, so nights with more than one film get adjoining rooms sharing a number and a porch light: 10A The Nice Guys, 10B Batman Begins, 10C The Dark Knight, all Friday July 24, three keys still in three doors under one bulb, which is what he actually called TRIPLE FEATURE FRIDAY. Nights he watched nothing are BLANK UNITS, physically present: July 20, July 22, July 25, July 28, August 5, August 7, 8 and 9 together, August 16, 19, 22, 23, 27, 28, 30. Fifteen of them. Stucco, an ice machine, a stack of white plastic chairs, and a taped index card from his night-debt list. Nine metres of nothing between Moon on August 6 and Predestination on August 10.

Score is not the geography. Score is the LIGHT. Every room's window glows at a brightness set by its score, so from the middle of the lot at 2am you read the entire board as a skyline without one number appearing anywhere: a blaze around the nines, a thin tail, and two nearly dead windows at 11B Malignant (5.4) and 15 Disclosure Day (5.2). The walkway floor carries the same information as pools of light, baked into a single canvas texture generated at load from the actual score array. The floor remembers the wall.

Specific rooms. ROOM 0 is the manager's room behind the office: the existing motel room, untouched, north wall of Polaroids, height is the score, exactly as it ships today. You start there and it is where score lives as an argument. THE OFFICE is the hub, the key rack, the laws, the register, the bell, and the exit. THE ANNEX is a second building across the lot behind a chainlink gate, unlit, which is the archive: 34 shoebox rooms with the develop mechanic exactly as built, and 30 drawer rooms whose door numbers have come off, furniture under dust sheets, no take on the pad because there is no take. THE FAR END past room 35 is 20 unbuilt units, foundations poured, room numbers spray-painted on plywood, one per queued title with his reason written on the plywood: PRISONERS, Villeneuve heavy excellent, needs weekend emotional budget.

And ROOM 4 is Memento. He watched it July 16, the fourth night, so chronology puts the 10.0 at door number 4 for free, and Memento is a motel room, and Memento.jsx already builds one. You spawn in Room 0 looking at a wall of photographs, you walk out and four doors down you open Room 4 and it is your room again, backwards.

**Why this shape.** The Court's instinct is right and its axis is wrong. Score is ALREADY argued, correctly and beautifully, by the north wall of Room 0. Building a second, larger score argument out of the whole property is the same sentence said twice and louder, and it turns the building into a ranked shelf, which is an archivist's geography, not a memory's. Memory is chronological and clustered by night. Only the time axis contains the July 24 triple, the July 17 triple, the No Country split watch across two nights, the five-night stretch in early August where nothing happened, and the fact that The Departed sits alone at room 28 with a dark night on either side of it. Those clusters are in the shipped data right now and the score axis cannot see any of them. One storey rather than five also means the whole board is visible from the middle of the lot, and it means no stairs, which is a mobility problem I would rather not design in than apologise for later.

**Archive and pitched.** The archive is the ANNEX, across the lot behind a chainlink gate that opens at 3:00am on the shift clock. Not because you earned it. Because that is when the auditor does the annex. Shoebox rooms (34) are faded and carry the develop transform exactly as fadedConfigFor and developedGradeFor already implement it, no change. Drawer rooms (30) have no number on the door and no pad by the phone, because there is nothing written, and that absence is the whole point of the drawer. The annex laundry and ice room carry all 45 quotes scratched into the wall by previous guests, 45 slots with visible gaps, which is the counter with no HUD: the twelve Selina Meyer lines are in the laundry and they are the funniest wall in the game.

The 78 PITCHED titles, entirely unrendered today, are the best unspent asset in the dataset and they get the office. They are the guests who never checked in. Seventy-eight reservation cards in the pigeonhole rack behind the desk, one instanced mesh, one draw call, each one naming the night it was offered and what beat it. Goodfellas is in that rack with a card that says he bailed and went to bed, and the next night he found Masters of the Universe by himself scrolling Prime and gave it 8.5. Apocalypse Now, Princess Mononoke, Beau Is Afraid and Suspiria are all in that rack against July 24, because he bought the Dark Knight trilogy by accident and started that instead. A pitched title is never a room. It was never a night.

**Wayfinding.** No minimap, no compass, no HUD, no quest marker. Five diegetic systems carry it. One, door numbers, lit, sequential, on every door, so you always know where you are on the summer. Two, the sign at the mouth of the lot: a backlit sun-faded plastic pylon, one tube out, THE VAULT and NO VACANCY, visible from anywhere in the lot and the only thing that is. Explicitly not neon and not a marquee, per the ban, and a plastic pylon is more right for this building anyway. Three, the light pools on the walkway, which make the nines physically pull you. Four, the clock: every room has a clock radio, they all agree, and the office window shows the shift time. Five, click-to-go, which is a first-class control and not an accessibility bolt-on: click any door from anywhere in the lot and you walk to it.

**Core loop.** You are on the walkway. You pick a door, by its number if you are walking the summer in order or by its window if you are chasing the bright ones. You go in and the film is running: the room is the scene, graded, lit, scored by its own synthesised recipe. You walk it, and as you get close to things his hot take assembles itself out of the room in three to six pieces, verbatim, one piece at a time. Halfway through you notice you cannot find the rest, so you flick the light switch by the door and the movie drains out of the room. The grade goes flat, the fog stops, the film's audio ducks away and you hear the air conditioner and the highway and the ice machine three doors down, and the set is still physically there but lit by a forty watt bulb and the parking lot through the blinds. Two more pieces are now readable that were not readable before, and one that was readable before is gone. You read the note taped to the connecting door, you go through it or you do not, you come out onto the walkway, and the shift clock has moved about fifteen minutes.\n\nWhat changes as a result: connecting doors unlock behind you, so the building gets more connected the more of it you have seen. Cards flip face up in the office as you complete the evidence for one of his 21 laws. The chainlink gate on the annex opens at 3. The sign loses a tube every hour. And the register in the office is writing a line about you the entire time, from your actual route and your actual dwell, which you do not see until the end.

**Verbs.**

- **Walk** — WASD, touch stick, or click anywhere and walk there. AABB slide collision as built, no physics engine. Head bob OFF by default. *(The leave-behind is placed so that the whole take is only readable from three or four different standing positions in the room. Walking is not traversal here, it is the reading mechanism. And click-to-go is a full second control scheme, not a toggle, so the entire game is playable with one thumb.)*
- **Look** — Mouse, drag, or gyro. FOV is fixed per room by its config and never yanked. *(Half the leave-behind fragments are angle dependent rather than distance dependent. The pen pressure on the top sheet of the pad only resolves at a raking angle, which is a real physical fact about paper and not a puzzle.)*
- **Read** — Passive. Get near a fragment and it resolves and joins the take in the corner of the screen. It never times out, never truncates, never edits him. *(It is the only interaction verb in the game and it is deliberately not a button. Nothing in this design should require a player to guess that a thing is interactive. If you can get to it, you have already read it.)*
- **Open** — Room doors on the walkway, connecting doors between adjoining films, the office, the annex gate. Doors swing, they do not fade. *(A connecting door between 11B Malignant and 8B Sorry to Bother You is forty metres of walkway that is also zero metres, and the note taped to it explains why. The bloodlines are the best writing in the dataset and today they are labels. This makes them architecture.)*
- **Switch** — The light switch by the door of every one of the 47 rooms. Turns the film off and the motel on, and back, as many times as you like. *(The signature. It doubles the content of every room for the cost of one config block, it makes the audio mean something, and it is the design's whole thesis in one gesture: the film is the dream and the motel is the fact, and both are in the room at the same time.)*
- **Hang** — In the annex only, on a drawer film's door: hang the DO NOT DISTURB tag. One press, no inventory, reversible. *(It is the only thing the player is allowed to assert, and it is deliberately not a score, because his numbers are not yours to write. Hanging the tag means this one deserves a night. The register counts them and names them back to you at dawn.)*
- **Ring** — The desk bell in the office. Once. Ends the shift and the visit. *(A game needs a player-owned ending, not a fade. You choose when the sun comes up. And a bell is the correct sound for finishing paperwork at six in the morning.)*

**Signature mechanic: THE HOUSE LIGHTS** (build cost M)

Every one of the 47 film rooms has a working light switch by the door, in the same place in every room, because underneath the film every room is still physically a motel room (see the room recipe). Flicking it lerps the room over about 700ms between two complete states. FILM state is what ships today: the per-room grade, fog, key and practical rig, and the film's synthesised audio recipe. MOTEL state is a shared preset plus a four-line per-room override: fog off, grade to neutral, one 2700K point light at the ceiling fixture, one cold spill through the blinds from the walkway, and the film's audio ducked to zero under a shared motel bed of air conditioner hum, highway, and the ice machine panned by its real distance down the walkway. The geometry never changes. The set is all still there, just unglamoured.

It is load bearing, not a toy. Of a room's three to six take fragments, two are only legible in one state and one is only legible in the other. In room 23 Barbarian the wine ring on the nightstand is invisible under the film's key and obvious under the bulb. In room 19A The Matrix the writing on the mirror only exists in the green. So you cannot finish a room without using the switch, and you learn that in the first room without being told.

*Why it is good.* It is the thesis of the whole design compressed into one gesture. This is a motel that remembers being other places, and the switch is the player physically toggling between the memory and the room the memory happened in. It doubles the content of all 47 rooms for a config block each. It solves the score-as-construction problem the recon flagged: Disclosure Day at 5.2 is a fully built, fully dressed room, and the difference is that when you flick its lights the film is not really missed, whereas flicking Blade Runner 2049's lights feels like turning off a fire. Nothing is built smaller, the switch just tells the truth about it. It gives audio its only semantic job in the game. And it is the one thing a person would describe to a friend in a sentence: it is the motel game where every room has a light switch that turns the movie off.

**How a room gets richer.** Every room, bespoke or templated, is built from five stacked layers. This is the recipe a builder works from.\n\nLAYER 1, THE SET. What ships today. The 16 bespoke rooms and the config-driven template engine, untouched. This is the good part and nothing here replaces it.\n\nLAYER 2, THE MOTEL UNDERNEATH. The single highest-value change in the design. Every film room is still, physically, a motel room, and the film's world is dressed over it. Same door in the same place, same window with the same bent blinds, same air conditioner rattling in the wall under it, same bathroom doorway, same popcorn ceiling, same one bad chair, same clock radio, same pad and pen by the phone, same light switch. Sicario's border room has the blinds. Blade Runner 2049's snowbound greenhouse has the air conditioner. Memento is the joke, because Memento already IS the room. Built once as a merged BufferGeometry and cloned per room, so it is 1 draw call and about 900 triangles, not 6 objects. What it buys: 47 disparate scenes become one building, the exit is always in the same place so you are never lost, and every room now has furniture at human scale to walk around rather than a scene to stand in front of.\n\nLAYER 3, THE LEAVE-BEHIND. Kill the three fixed self-lit info planes. The hot take breaks into three to six fragments and each one lives on a real object where a person would actually have left it: pen pressure on the top sheet of the pad because the top sheet is torn off, breath on the mirror, a phrase around the rim of a paper cup, the TV still on its pause screen, the DO NOT DISTURB card on the inside handle, the corner of a takeout menu. Placed at opposite ends of the room and at different heights, so the only way to get the whole thing is to walk the whole room. It is cheaper than what ships now, because it is the same text-on-a-plane pipeline moved onto props that already exist, and it converts a room from viewable to explorable without hiding a single thing. Nothing is behind anything. Everything is visible from the doorway. You just cannot read it from there.\n\nLAYER 4, THE NIGHT ITSELF. Every film carries a context line and they are pure gold and completely unrendered today. They become physical evidence, never text. Room 23 Barbarian, wine night, early night: one glass, one ring, and the DO NOT DISTURB card is on the wrong side of the door. Room 13 Annihilation, wiped after six hours of rehearsal: bed not turned down, shoes still by the door, TV is the only light in the room, which is why it is darker than its neighbours. Room 19B Pressure, accidental late-night watch on Peacock autoplay: the remote is on the floor and nobody chose this room. Room 16 The Sting, benched twice before it happened: three holes in the paint by the door hook. Room 15 Disclosure Day, daytime watch: it is the only room in the entire Vault where it is the afternoon behind the blinds, and it is also the worst film on the board, and that is funnier left alone. Room 27 Rogue One has no context line, so it is a clean room with a made bed. Absence is information too.\n\nAnd the rule that governs all of Layer 4: THE VAULT NEVER EDITORIALISES A NIGHT. Room 28 is The Departed. Its context line says divorce papers were signed earlier that day. What is in the room is a hotel pen and a stack of paper turned face down and squared to the table edge, and nothing else. The register card by the door carries the date and his words verbatim, and the Vault says not one thing about them. The place remembers. It does not comment. That is the difference between this design and a grief game, and it is a hard rule, not a preference.\n\nLAYER 5, THE CONNECTING DOORS. Two to five per room, one per bloodline, with the note taped to it in his voice at reading height.\n\nDraw call budget for all five layers: set about 18, underlay 1, leave-behind about 2 net new, night evidence 2 to 4, doors 1 instanced. Twenty-four to twenty-six, inside the thirty.

**Progression.** Nothing is gated by count, skill, or repetition. Three ladders, each gated by something honest.\n\nONE, THE CONNECTING DOORS. Every bloodline is a physical connecting door in both of its rooms, 39 of them. A connecting door starts deadbolted from the other side. It unlocks the moment you have stood in the room on the other side, in either order. The note is taped to it and readable through the gap before it opens, so you always know what you are missing and why. This means the building gets more connected the more of it you walk, which is progression driven purely by curiosity. You cannot grind it and you cannot fail it.\n\nTWO, THE LAWS. All 21 taste laws are cards in the office, face down in a rack. A card flips when you have stood in every room it cites as evidence. The antibodies law flips on The Game, Memento and The Sting. The reveal-as-event law flips on Malignant and Sorry to Bother You, and it is the one that will make a stranger say out loud that they get it. The thin-ensemble law flips on Annihilation, Sunshine and Coherence. Flipping a card is not a reward for effort, it is the game saying here is what all that walking added up to, and it is the single strongest reason a stranger stays: they are not learning facts about a man, they are learning how to argue about films, with one guy's homework as the worked example.\n\nTHREE, THE CLOCK. Shift time starts at 12:00am and advances roughly fifteen minutes per room whose take you finish. At 3:00am the chainlink on the annex opens. At 6:00am the sky starts going grey, the sign goes out, and the office door unlocks. Critically the clock is an OFFER and not a limit. Dawn does not arrive on its own and the sun does not rise until you walk into the office and ring the bell. You can keep walking at 6:00am forever. Nothing expires.

**The ending.** You walk into the office. Behind the desk is the key rack with 78 reservation cards in it, the law cards, and the door to Room 0. On the desk the register is open to tonight's page, and there is one line on it, and the line is about you. The Vault has been writing it the whole time out of your real route and your real dwell, in the voice of somebody filling out a shift report at six in the morning and slightly enjoying himself:\n\n"Night of the 4th. One guest, no name. Went straight to 11B and stayed nine minutes, which is longer than the movie deserved. Did the whole east run and never opened a single connecting door. Read Coherence twice. Turned the house lights on in Sicario and left them on. Longest stop, room 28, four minutes at the window. Fourteen takes, three laws, one tag hung on Apollo 13. Nobody went back to 4."\n\nThen the one thing on the desk that was not there when you arrived: a reservation card, filled out, for you. It is one of his 154 recommendations, picked by matching where you actually stood against the vibe tags of the rooms you stood in, and it carries his own reasoning verbatim, not a generated blurb. If you spent your night in the loop rooms it is Predestination's neighbours. If you spent it in the annex it is something from the hazy wing, because his own law says his bad memory is an asset and a long-gap rewatch plays like a first watch. One card. Not a list, not a percentage, not a completion bar, and there is no completion bar anywhere in this game.\n\nYou ring the bell. **Inclusivity by design.** Not an options menu. Seven design decisions.\n\nTHE DOOR IS THE CONTENT WARNING. Every door on the walkway has a card in a holder beside it: title, year, runtime, and the film's vibe tags verbatim from his own data. Room 25 Hereditary reads dread, slow-burn, grief-horror, shock, endurance-event, and underneath it his line, WHAT THE FUCK MAN. Anxiety is not excitement, and I am not doing the homework. You decide at the door, in his words, before you are inside. This is a content warning that is also characterisation, it is diegetic, and it covers everything, not just horror: room 20 Moon is one-man-show and space-thriller, room 29 Masters of the Universe is camp and queer-read, and subtitles appear as a thing he avoids, which quietly normalises the idea that everybody has a lane.\n\nLEAVING COSTS NOTHING AND IS ALWAYS ONE INPUT. Because Layer 2 puts the same motel under every room, the door is in the same place in all 47 of them, always behind you and to the left. Plus Escape, B, or back walks you straight out from anywhere. Everything you read is already kept in the register, so you can leave a room in the middle of a sentence, forever, and lose nothing. That is the concrete answer to opting out without losing progress.\n\nNO-FAIL, NO TIMER, NOTHING MISSABLE. There is no lose state, no death, no chase, no precision input, no timing challenge, and no thing that can be permanently missed. The shift clock only moves forward, never runs out, and the exit is available from the first minute. If you stand still for four seconds the nearest unread fragment gets a soft ring, so nobody is ever stuck without knowing it.\n\nTHE VERB SET IS SEVEN AND THE GAME TEACHES NONE OF THEM. No inventory, no resources, no crafting, no combat, no menus you must learn, no map to read. Reading is passive. The only two things you press are a light switch and a door.\n\nREADING LOAD IS BOUNDED BY CONSTRUCTION. No wall of text ever appears. A take arrives in three to six pieces, one at a time, at reading distance, at a size set by distance and not by a slider, and it stays until you walk away. His takes run long and profane and that is exactly why they get broken up: a paragraph of caps in eight point on a card is not readable by anyone.\n\nMOTION DEFAULTS TO CALM. Head bob off by default rather than on, camera never yanked, FOV never pulled, no forced turns, and the entire game runs at walking pace by design. That is a design fact rather than an accessibility setting, which means it is true for everybody without anyone having to go find it.\n\nA STRANGER GETS THE BETTER DEAL. The register line at dawn is written about the visitor's own route, not about Dixon, so a stranger leaves with a portrait of their own night. And the laws are the bridge: flipping an unguessable IDEA is not the same as an unguessable REVEAL teaches a genuinely useful thing about how movies work, using Malignant at 5.4 and Sorry to Bother You at 9.4 as the worked example. A stranger does not stay for a man they have never met. They stay because the building taught them how to argue about films.

**Three moments that sell it.**

**What it sacrifices.** Five real things, no hedging.\n\nSPECTACLE. There is no chase, no combat, no timing challenge, no set piece, no fail state and no adrenaline anywhere in this game. ## NO VACANCY

**Pitch.** You check into a motel at dusk. The office is closed, there is a key in a wire basket on the counter, and the whole building is one man's year of movies. Every film he has scored is a numbered door: the number on the door is the score. Room 52 is Disclosure Day. Room 100 is Memento and it is alone on the roof. You walk exterior breezeways in the dark, you put your ear to doors, you let yourself into rooms, and each room is a motel room that a film has happened to. There is a courtyard with a drained pool, a laundry with the house rules taped up, an ice machine that hums all night, a basement full of boxes, and a vacancy sign on a pole by the road. You have until dawn, which is not a threat, it is just when the night ends. Nothing chases you and nothing can be failed. The pleasure is that the place is real, continuous, and for one night, yours.

**Who the player is.** You are a guest. Not him, not a detective, not a curator. You pulled off the road, the office was dark, and there was a key out for you. That framing does three things a stranger needs. It explains why you are somewhere personal without being invited into a diary. It makes snooping the correct behavior, because a motel room is a place you are supposed to open the drawers of. And it puts you on the right side of the one line that matters: Room 4, ground floor by the office, is the owner's own room and it is the only room in the building you are trespassing in. Everything else is a guest room. Dixon lives here. You are staying here. The building never tells you who he is and never needs to, because you spend the night in his handwriting.

**Verdict on The Court:** adopt-with-changes

**Structure.** A three-wing U motel around a courtyard, opening south to the road, four storeys plus a roof, with the office at the southwest corner and Room 4 next to it. Courtyard is 30m by 22m, walkway perimeter around the U is 82 metres. Every floor has the same footprint, which is the first change from the proposal: the building does not grow as you climb, it gets DENSER. Score band selects the floor and position along the 82-metre perimeter is the fractional score, anchored at the office corner. Ground floor covers 5.0 to 6.9 across the full 82m and holds five doors: 52 Disclosure Day at 8.2m, 54 Malignant at 16.4m, then a fifty-three-metre blank, then 67 Hereditary and 67A Under the Silver Lake at 69.7m and 68 The Game at 73.8m. Floor two is the sevens, ten doors, the first thirty-three metres empty and then three touching doors at 74 (Annihilation, Oblivion, Sunshine) and three more at 79 (Obsession, Source Code, Triangle). Floor three is the eights, ten doors, evenly spread, the most normal-looking motel floor in the building. Floor four is the nines, twenty-one doors, crowded and clustered, with five doors touching frame to frame at 96 (Enemy, L.A. Confidential, Se7en, The Dark Knight Rises, The Sting) and four touching at 98 (Blade Runner 2049, One Battle After Another, The Dark Knight, The Matrix), and it is the only floor you can walk as a continuous loop. The roof has one door, Room 100, Memento, plus the back of the vacancy sign and the ice machine's compressor. Ties get letters and touching frames, so Sicario and The Departed are 99 and 99A with no wall between them. Around the doors: the office (pegboard of all 47 hooks behind the counter as the map, the ledger book with 51 session notes as dated pages, five mail slots holding the mailbox notes, the wire key basket, the bell), the laundry in the north wing (six breakers, one per floor, and the 21 taste laws taped up in a plastic sleeve as GUEST POLICIES), the vending alcove (an ice machine with the nine night-debt items scratched inside its lid, and a drink machine whose 45 slots are the quotes, mostly empty, the gap in the rack being the only counter in the game), the north stairwell and a service lift, the basement (the archive, with the develop mechanic living in a laundry sink under a red bulb), the drained pool with the score axis painted in the deep end and the 8.55 average as a high-water stain on the wall, and the parking lot with 78 numbered spaces, each one carrying a faded stencil of a film that was pitched and passed on.

**Why this shape.** The proposal's real insight is that a room number is a score that nobody reads as data, and that survives intact. What I changed is the growth rule, because an inverted ziggurat cannot be sold as a real motel and the whole design depends on the place being believable. Constant footprint with variable door density does the same job better: the ground floor is mostly wall and the fourth floor is mostly door, which is the score distribution rendered as architecture, readable from the courtyard in one glance, in no language. The 82-metre perimeter is chosen so that a 0.1 score step is 8.2 metres, which is far enough that adjacent scores are separate places you walk between and close enough that ties can touch. The blank stretches are not dead space, they are the argument: he does not watch bad movies and the bottom of this building barely exists. The bigger change is what a film room IS. The proposal keeps the portal idea; I am killing it. The film rooms are motel rooms that a film has happened to, with the motel's own bones visible in every one of them, which is what makes this a building you live in rather than a hub with forty-seven wormholes in it. It costs the rooms some scale and it buys total coherence, and coherence is the entire pitch.

**Archive and pitched.** The Shoebox (34 remembered in pencil) and the Dark Drawer (30 seen but unscorable) go to the basement, reached by the north stairwell or the service lift. Basement is storage, storage is things not on the books, and the existing develop mechanic lives there as a darkroom in the laundry sink under a red bulb, which is where it always belonged. The basement is also where the quote cards are, in the boxes, which is why you go down there twice: once to browse and once because the vending rack upstairs has gaps in it. The 78 pitched titles are the parking lot. Seventy-eight numbered spaces, every one empty, each with a name stencilled on the asphalt and faded, and the pass reason underneath. You walk the lot at the start and again at the end and it is a list of everything that almost happened, and it costs zero draw calls because the entire lot is one generated ground texture. The 20 queued films fill the blank stretches: in the gaps between doors on every floor there are doors with no number yet, hung with housekeeping tags, being made up for tomorrow, with the queue's rank and where-to-watch on the tag. That is what turns the empty walls from dead space into anticipation.

**Wayfinding.** No map, no minimap, no HUD, no compass. Four systems instead. The courtyard is visible from every walkway and through every room's window, so any moment of confusion is fixed by turning your head. The office window is the only warm light at ground level and it never goes out, so south is always findable. Door numbers ARE the coordinate system, since 52 through 100 tells you floor and position at once and the numbering runs the same direction on every floor. And the doors you have opened stay open and stay lit, so the far wing across the courtyard is a permanent visible record of where you have already been, at range, at night, without any interface at all.

**Core loop.** You are on a dark exterior walkway with a chest-high concrete wall on one side and a row of numbered doors on the other. You walk. You pass a door and read its number, which is its score, and see the color of the light under it. You hold to listen and the room's own sound comes through the wood, muffled and low. If you have the key, the knob turns. Inside, the room is dark. You find the switch by the door, then the bed lamp, then the bathroom, and each one shows you a different third of the room. You open the nightstand drawer and find a piece of motel stationery with one fragment of his hot take on it. You go around the corner to the bathroom, which you could not see from the doorway, and the mirror has his verdict written in it. You sit on the bed and the TV comes on with the plot. You open the closet and there are hangers with other rooms' keys on them, one for each film this film is related to. You take a key, or you do not. You come out and leave the door open behind you, because open doors stay open, and now that room's light is falling on the walkway and across the courtyard onto the far wing. What changes: the courtyard gets brighter every time you open a door, the key ring in your hand gets longer and louder, the second word of the vacancy sign lights up letter by letter, the taped-up house rules in the laundry become readable as you visit the films they cite, and the sky moves toward dawn whether you use the night or not.

**Verbs.**

- **Walk** — WASD, arrows, touch stick, or hold-one-button-and-steer-with-look as a first-class scheme. No run. No jump. No crouch. Flat walkways, one staircase, one lift. *(The building is the content, so covering ground IS the game. Removing run keeps the fifty-three metres of blank stucco on the ground floor honest, because that emptiness is a real fact about his taste and it should take real time to cross.)*
- **Look** — Pointer lock, touch drag, arrow-key look. Aim is also the interaction cursor: whatever is centered and within reach is what you act on. *(One cursor for the whole game means no reticle clutter and no separate select verb. It also means the courtyard, visible from every walkway and every room window, is always available as a compass just by turning your head.)*
- **Listen** — Hold the action button while facing a door. The camera eases six inches toward the wood and that room's Web Audio bed rises through a lowpass at 380Hz. Works on every door, including ones you have no key for. *(This is the whole design's escape valve. It gives 47 doors a personality before you commit to any of them, it turns a corridor of doors into a corridor of places, and it means the entire building is playable at arm's length by someone who does not want to be in a horror room.)*
- **Open and close** — Doors, the nightstand drawer, the closet, curtains, the ice machine lid, the breaker panel, the office after-hours drawer, basement boxes. One contextual button, physical hinges, real time, no snap. *(It is the immersive-sim tell and it is the content delivery system at the same time. Every piece of writing in this game is behind something that swings or slides. Nothing floats.)*
- **Take and hang** — Keys go on the ring. Quote cards go into the vending rack. That is the entire inventory. Two things, both visible in your hand, neither in a menu. *(A deliberately tiny inventory keeps the cognitive load near zero while still giving the building an economy. The ring is also the save file and the ending's input: at dawn the clerk reads it.)*
- **Switch** — Room lights (three per room: door, bed lamp, bathroom), walkway lights, the pool light, the vacancy sign, and six breakers in the laundry. *(Light stops being a mood setting and becomes a thing you operate. Three switches per room means one room is three different rooms. Killing the walkway breaker is the only way to see the pool floor properly, so darkness is a tool and not a punishment.)*
- **Sit** — The bed in any room, the two pool chairs, the stool behind the office counter. Sitting starts the TV, which is where the long writing lives. *(It gates every paragraph over forty words behind a seated, stationary, opt-in posture. Nobody ever has to read a wall of text while standing in a doorway wondering if something is behind them.)*
- **Read** — Face a surface within a metre and the camera pushes in. The mirror, the stationery, the policy sign, the ledger book, the letters stencilled on the parking lot. No modal, no overlay, no page turn. *(Text stays in the world. It also gives the design its one honest way to render his hot takes verbatim, profanity and typos intact, without a UI card: it is a note somebody actually wrote and left in a drawer.)*

**Signature mechanic: Ear to the Door** (build cost S)

Every one of the 47 doors is a complete free preview of its film, from the walkway, without a key and without entering. Four channels, all diegetic, all always on. The NUMBER stamped on the plate is the score, 52 through 100, and a motel room number is the one place in the world where a number on a wall is not a UI element. The LIGHT under the door is that room's palette and its state of construction, a hot narrow bar of amber under 98 and a flat wide slab of fluorescent white under 52. HOLDING the action button leans you into the wood and brings the room's existing synth recipe up through a BiquadFilter lowpass at 380Hz with gain around 0.15, so Hereditary's room breathes at you and Baby Driver's ticks. And the KNOB tells you instantly, by turning or not turning, whether that key is on your ring. Nothing in this system ever lies, nothing in it can startle you, and no door has a jump behind it.

*Why it is good.* It solves the single biggest risk in a building made of doors, which is that a corridor of forty-seven doors is a menu with hinges. Listening turns each door into a place before you have committed a step to it, and the score number stops being data because it is a room number. It is also, without one line of options-menu copy, the inclusivity spine: a player who does not want to walk into a grief-horror room can spend the entire night in the breezeways getting the true temperature of all 47 films and never open anything, and that is a complete visit, not a lesser one. And it is the immersive-sim texture that makes the place feel real. Real motels have thin doors.

**How a room gets richer.** Five fixtures, in all 47 rooms, no exceptions, imposed by the motel on top of whatever world the film brought with it. This is the thesis: the rooms do not replace the motel, they are what happened to the motel. Sicario's tunnel is a tunnel that has come up through the floor of room 99. The nightstand drawer holds one fragment of the hot take on Discount-grade motel stationery in his handwriting, verbatim, typos and profanity intact. The bathroom mirror carries the verdict paragraph, written in the condensation, legible only within a metre. The closet holds a hanger per bloodline with that film's key on it. The window looks out on the courtyard from this room's exact height and angle, so you are always located in the building and can never be lost. The TV is off until you sit on the bed, and then it is the plot paragraph. Four hard rules make the box explorable instead of viewable. One: every room is an L, with the bathroom and its mirror around a corner you cannot see from the doorway, which costs two wall planes and kills the "shows you everything from the doorway" problem in all forty-seven rooms at once. Two: the room is dark when you enter and has three separate switches, so a single room is three rooms. Three: the curtains start closed, which makes opening them a fourth light and the moment the film's world and the motel's night meet in the same frame. Four: the three floating self-lit info planes are deleted outright. Nothing floats anymore. Budget: the fixture kit is about 13 draw calls and 6k triangles (alcove 1 merged, vanity and mirror 2, nightstand and drawer 2, closet with instanced hangers 2, TV and bed 2, window and curtain 2, three lamps 3), which leaves 17 draw calls and 34k triangles for the film's own world, more than any of the 16 bespoke rooms currently spend. It pays for itself by deleting InfoSurfaces. As for a 5.2 still being a place: room 52, Disclosure Day, is fully built and fully dressed, same fixtures, same five interactions, same three switches. What makes it a 5.2 is not that less exists, it is that everything in it is on at once. Overhead fluorescent, no lamp warmth, no shadow, curtains already open, nothing withheld, the whole room legible from the threshold in one flat sweep. It is a complete motel room that has told you everything before you walked in, which is exactly and only what he docked the film for.

**Progression.** Geography is never gated. From minute one you can climb the north stairwell to the roof and stand outside Room 100, or take the service lift to the basement. Doors are gated by keys, and keys come from three places. First, the office counter. The pegboard with all 47 hooks is behind the counter where you cannot reach it, which makes it the map. On the counter is a wire basket that always holds exactly one key, and it is always the next film in his real watch chronology that you have not been handed. The first key is always 98, Blade Runner 2049, July 13. Forty-seven return trips walks you through his year in the order he lived it. Second, closets. Every room's closet holds a hanger for each of that film's 35 bloodlines, and on each hanger is that room's key. Sicario, room 99, is the hub: its closet has Blade Runner 2049 (98), Nightcrawler (94), No Country for Old Men (83), and The Sting (96), all four hanging on the villeneuve and execution-meritocracy lines. Barbarian's closet, room 76, has bare hangers and nothing on them, because Barbarian connects to nothing on his board, and an empty closet is a true statement. The two orders are the point: the office is chronology, the closets are affinity, and the building itself is quality. You choose which one you travel by and you can switch at any door. The best thing this produces is real and comes straight out of his data: room 68, The Game at 6.8, sits at the far dark end of the ground floor and looks like the least promising door in the building, and inside its closet on the antibodies line is the key to Room 100. Third, at dawn the office's after-hours drawer unlocks and every key is in it. Nothing is permanently missable and nothing is earned by repetition or reflex. Separately, the 21 house rules taped up in the laundry unlock by evidence: a rule becomes legible when you have opened or listened at every room it cites. Rule 3, the one about an unguessable IDEA not being an unguessable REVEAL, needs room 54 and room 94, Malignant and Sorry to Bother You, and those two are connected by a closet key, so the chain that proves the rule is a walk you can actually take from the bottom of the building to the top.

**The ending.** Dawn arrives on its own, roughly forty minutes in. It does not evict you. The sky over the courtyard goes from black to the flat grey-blue of five in the morning, the walkway lights click off one bank at a time on their photocell, the vacancy sign's ballast cuts and it goes dark mid-buzz, and the office window turns yellow. Rooms you are inside stay night, because the clock is outside only and their grades are authored. When you walk into the office there is a figure behind the counter now, abstracted, no face, just a shape with its weight on one hip. You put the ring on the counter. He looks at it, and at what you did with the night, and slides one index card across. It is one of the 154 recommendations, picked from your actual route: which floor you spent your time on, which rooms you dwelled in, which chains you followed, and which doors you only listened at. If you spent the night on the fourth floor and kept going back to the doors that scared you, the card reads: "You stayed upstairs and you kept opening the ones that made noise. Watch Prisoners. Villeneuve, heavy, excellent. Needs a weekend emotional budget, so do not start it at midnight." Under the recommendation is the list of every room number you opened, which is a list of scores, which is your night written as his handwriting. Then you push the office door, cross the lot past the seventy-eight faded stencils of films that were pitched and passed on, and the road is there. You leave with a movie to watch and a number you understand.

**Inclusivity by design.** Ear to the Door is the load-bearing one: the whole building is playable at arm's length, and content warnings are diegetic and honest, because the door that has dread behind it sounds like dread from the walkway and never surprises you. No-fail is structural, not a difficulty setting. There is no timer, no failure, no enemy, no reflex input, no way to lose a key, and dawn does not evict you. You can leave any room instantly, and the door you came in through is always behind you with courtyard light in it, in the same place relative to the room, in all forty-seven. Opting out of a room never costs progress, because at dawn the after-hours drawer holds every key in the building, so nothing is ever locked behind a room you did not want to be in. Wayfinding needs no map, no HUD and no minimap: the courtyard is visible from every walkway and every room window, and the office light is the only warm point at ground level and never goes out. Cognitive load is one vocabulary learned once. Five fixtures in the same five places in all 47 rooms means you learn the motel and then you can read any film in it, so the ninth room is easier than the first rather than another thing to decode. Reading is tiered by posture: the headline is on the wall at a glance, the body is in your hand when you pick the card up, and paragraphs over forty words only ever appear on a TV you have to sit down for. Movement is flat: no jump, no run, no crouch, no timed input, and the service lift in the north wing is a real place with a real reason to exist so the stairwell is never required. The walkway edge is a solid chest-high concrete wall rather than a rail, so looking down four storeys is a choice you make by leaning, not a thing the game does to you. A stranger versus the owner is handled by the room assignment: Room 4 is his and everything else is a guest room, so a visitor is a guest here, not an intruder in somebody's diary, and Dixon arriving finds his own Polaroid wall exactly where he left it. And the one that matters most, the building explains the archive without language at all. Door density is the score distribution. You look up at the front of the motel and the top floor is a solid unbroken run of touching doors and the ground floor is a hundred and eighty feet of empty stucco, and you understand a stranger's taste in three seconds without reading a word of English.

**Three moments that sell it.**

- You step out of Room 4 onto the ground floor walkway at dusk and turn left. Two doors near you, 52 and 54, Disclosure Day and Malignant, and then fifty-three metres of blank stucco with nothing on it at all, and then three doors bunched at the far dark corner, 67, 67A and 68. You look up. The fourth floor is a solid unbroken row of doors all the way around the courtyard, five of them touching at 96 with no wall between the frames, four more touching at 98, and above all of it one light on the roof. Nobody has said the word score. You already know everything.
- Room 68, The Game, 6.8, at the far end of the ground floor past all that empty wall, is the least promising door in the building and it looks it. Inside, in the closet, on a single hanger on the antibodies line, is the key to Room 100. You climb four flights and a roof ladder in the dark with it. On the roof there is nothing but the back of the vacancy sign, the ice machine's compressor, and one door with a number on it a motel cannot have. Inside is a motel room. Memento is a motel room. It is Room 4 with the geometry reversed and somebody else's Polaroids on the wall, and when you open the nightstand drawer the piece of stationery in it reads, in his handwriting, MY GOD WHAT THE FUCKKKKK BRO.
- Two in the morning, and it starts raining on the courtyard. You are on the fourth-floor breezeway on the north wing with eleven doors open behind you along the east side, and you look across the yard and the far wing is a row of lit rectangles hanging in the dark at the height of the ninety-somethings, with the rain crossing through the spill. Down below, the courtyard is bright enough now that you can finally read the deep end of the drained pool, where the score axis is painted on the concrete and the stain on the wall marks the waterline at 8.55. Behind you, over the road, the second word of the vacancy sign is most of the way lit.

**What it sacrifices.** Instant access, and that is the big one. Today a film is one click. Here a film is a walk, sometimes a long walk in the dark, and some visitors will bounce in the first ninety seconds because they wanted the wall of Polaroids and got a parking lot. Curated pacing goes too. A sequence design can build a crescendo and guarantee that the first thing you see is the best thing; a place cannot, and somebody's entire impression of this project will be formed by wandering into Room 52 first. Scale is the real artistic sacrifice. Making the motel's bones visible inside every film room is the thesis, and the price is a hard cap on how far a room can travel from being a room. The Matrix rooftop and the Blade Runner 2049 wastes lose real size when they have to fit around a nightstand and a bathroom alcove, and I am choosing coherence over spectacle on purpose. Authoring cost is enormous and I will not pretend otherwise: 47 rooms times five fixtures is 235 pieces of hand-placed content on top of the 16 bespoke rooms that already exist, plus a full exterior. It is also slow. A complete visit is forty to fifty minutes and there is no version of this that is a three-minute experience. And it gives up the bloodline graph as a browsable object. The 35 links become closet hangers, which is more physical and more useful, but you can never stand back and see the shape of the whole web the way a graph view would let you.

**First thing to build.** The ground floor at dusk, and nothing else. Build the west wing exterior from the office corner to the far dark corner, at true measured spacing off the real scores: Room 4 by the office, then 52 at 8.2 metres, 54 at 16.4, then fifty-three metres of blank stucco, then 67, 67A and 68 bunched at 69.7 to 73.8. Build the courtyard with the drained pool and the vacancy sign, because the walkway is meaningless without something to look out over. Get four things working on all five doors: the number plates from one generated atlas canvas, the light under the door, Ear to the Door with the existing recipes through a lowpass, and doors that swing on real hinges and stay exactly how you left them with their spill falling on the walkway. Then fit exactly one room completely, with all five fixtures, three switches, the L-shaped bathroom alcove, and a working closet: Room 67, Hereditary. Pick Hereditary specifically because it is a 6.7 and it is the test that matters. The kill criteria are two and they are answerable in about two weeks. One: is walking from 54 to 67 in the dark, past fifty-three metres of wall with nothing on it, interesting or is it just long. If it is just long, the whole spatial thesis is dead and you have lost two weeks instead of four months. Two: does Hereditary at 6.7, with only the five-fixture armature and no bespoke world at all, feel like a place worth three minutes. If the armature alone carries a mid-scoring film, it carries all forty-seven and the bespoke rooms become gravy instead of the entire budget.

Three specific things won it. First, score stops being architecture and becomes position in the running order, which is how I actually judge work. The motel stays byte-identical and becomes the set of Memento, because Memento is a motel room and my wall of 47 Polaroids hanging in it is diegetically perfect as the note wall. **Fatal flaws called:**

This design will look bad in the middle of its build in a way the current dioramas never do, and there is no month where it is half good. It is either 12 finished numbers or it is nothing.
- *THE SECOND WATCH*: Ten lit doors a night with a nightly reroll is a live-service instinct dropped into a personal archive, and the flat strip is a world it decided not to build. It has the best mechanics in the pile sitting in the thinnest place of the six.
- *NO VACANCY*: Putting the motel's bones inside all 47 rooms caps the scale of the sixteen best rooms I already have. Blade Runner 2049 and the Matrix rooftop lose real size to fit around a nightstand. Also the most expensive thing in it is 200 invisible law-diagnosis mappings, and if those are done lazily the whole mechanic reads as random.
- *THE NIGHT CATALOGUE*: It asks a stranger to deduce a subjective number and then refuses to tell them which of three blanks was wrong. Obra Dinn works because the answers are facts. Mine are opinions, and being told you filed my opinion incorrectly is not a puzzle, it is a scolding. The KEEPER ribbon is the design admitting out loud that it does not work for the person whose collection it is, which is a tell.

**What every design missed.** Every one of these six designed a finished monument to a thing that gets one film longer every few nights, and none of them designed for film 48.

That is the only fact about this project that is actually true. I watch something, I write the take that night, the ritual writes it to Supabase. The archive is not a corpus of 47, it is a habit that has run 47 times so far. And all six of these authored 47 bespoke somethings: 47 cue stacks, 47 reveal positions, 47 held seconds, 47 sets of indices, 235 hand-placed fixtures. On the night I score number 48, every one of these worlds has a hole in it and needs a person with taste to sit down and hand-author a room before the site is honest again. The Night Audit is the only document that even notices, and it notices as a constraint on the walkway layout, not as a design problem.

Two halves to the miss, and they are the same miss.

The first half is the growth mechanic. There is no design here for the room that builds itself the night the take lands. Nobody asked what the minimum authored payload is for a new film to become a real place: which fields the ritual would have to write so the engine can stage a number from the take alone, and what the honest in-between state looks like while it waits for me. That state should be a designed thing, not a gap. A number on the standby list, taped out on the deck under worklights, on the board with a date and no cues hung yet. Somebody who visits the night after I watch something should see that it happened and see that it is not staged yet, and that should look deliberate.

The second half is worse. Nobody gave me a verb. Read the six player fictions back: a night cataloguer, a night auditor, an appraiser, an audience member, a motel guest, a motel guest. All six built for a stranger and then handed me a mode toggle. A ribbon on a desk that turns the deduction off. A room with my name on it. The KEEPER switch is the tell, because it is a design saying the only way to be the owner here is to turn the game off.

I am the person who will open this most, and the moment that matters is 1am, film just finished, take just written, and I want to go look at what my own building did about it. Not play it. Look at it. Nobody designed that thirty seconds. The set going up, the card going on the board, the number finding its place in the corridor, whatever moved because a new number needed the room. That is the loop that has actually run 47 times, and six designs went past it to build a museum about the results.

- EAR TO THE DOOR, from NO VACANCY. Hold the action button facing any closed door and that room's existing synth recipe comes through the wood at a 380Hz lowpass. S cost. In Ghost Light it goes on the dressing room corridor and the lobby cases, so every number is previewable before you commit a step, and it is the diegetic content warning that never announces itself as one.
- THE DOOR NUMBER IS THE SCORE, from NO VACANCY. Ghost Light has no physical expression of the number anywhere and that is its one real hole. - TWO DOORS OUT, EACH PAINTED WITH A REAL LINE, from THE SECOND WATCH. Every number ends in the wings through one of two doors, each painted in the film's own palette with something I actually said about it. Neither is correct. Zero UI, zero prompt, and it is how the ending gets its data.

**Winner: THE SECOND WATCH.** It is the only design whose scope is a ramp instead of a cliff, and on this codebase that is the whole ballgame.

Measured reasons, in build order.

The world is nearly free. A flat strip of 47 instanced door slabs, one canvas atlas for the numerals, one walkway, one lot. Roughly twelve draw calls, and DoorRow.jsx and doors.js already exist to do it. Every other design requires a multi-storey building with stairs, ramps, lifts, basements, mezzanines and wings to exist before its mechanic can be tested even once. The Night Catalogue and No Vacancy both need a month of architecture before anyone can tell whether the game is fun.

The signature mechanic is already shipping. The second state is the develop transform generalised. fadedConfigFor and developedGradeFor already prove an invertible transform over a shared config is the right shape here, and it is already load bearing in the archive. Second Watch points the same proven trick at every room: same geometry, same draw calls, a second grade object and a second lightRig preset plus a visibility mask. Zero new meshes, zero new triangles, and the risky part of the pipeline is code that has already survived QA.

Ten lit doors solves the problem nobody else even names. Every other design needs all 47 rooms authored to its new standard before it is coherent: 47 held seconds, 47 rooms that argue a number, 235 fixtures, 47 choreographies, 47 motel underlays. On one developer that is the thing that kills the project in month four. Ten lit doors makes the room count a content pipeline. Ship with ten finished rooms, add one a week forever, and the rotation makes an incomplete game read as a curated night rather than an unfinished build. It also buys the only genuine reason to return that anybody proposed, at zero content cost.

It dodges the data cliff by accident but it dodges it. It is the one design that explicitly rejects taste-law progression, calling it a wiki with a fetch quest attached. That refusal is also the only thing standing between it and the missing lesson-evidence join described below, which is the largest unbudgeted item across the other five.

The vertical slice is honest and cheap. Malignant into Sorry to Bother You behind a query flag on the existing motel, where Stby.jsx already exists as a 585 line bespoke room. That slice measures both numbers that decide whether 47 rooms is a plan or a fantasy: what it costs to retrofit a second state onto a finished bespoke room, and what it costs to build a new one to standard. No other design's first slice measures anything that falsifiable for that little money.

Score-as-attention is the cheapest content system in the batch. The room notices you at 9.5, the room ignores you at 7.4, the room talks over you below 7.0. That is a config field routed through systems that already exist. It also solves the trap the brief flagged: nothing is built smaller for a low score, the room is just uninterested in you, which is funny, true to his actual complaints, and means the bottom of the wall becomes somewhere people go on purpose.

What it is weakest at is context, and that is fixable with grafts rather than a redesign. It is not the most beautiful proposal on the table. No Vacancy is. It is the one that gets built.

**Fatal flaws called:**

- *THE SECOND WATCH*: The first watch has zero Dixon in it, and 90 seconds in a cold room with no voice, no number and no context can read as empty rather than as withheld. 'More context' was an explicit ask and this design defers context behind a reveal the player might not find. The 90-second auto-fire patches it mechanically but not emotionally. Second exposure: 47 hand-found reveal positions is real taste work no script can do.
- *THE NIGHT AUDIT*: Layer 2, the motel underneath every room, is a retrofit into 16 bespoke rooms that have hundreds of QA lessons baked into their constants and camera aims. Sicario and BR2049 will fight an air conditioner and a bent blind, and the failure mode is making the best rooms in the project worse. The design also quietly needs the same missing lesson-evidence join for its law cards.
- *NO VACANCY*: Stated scope is four storeys, three wings, an 82m perimeter, a stairwell, a lift, a basement, a laundry with six breakers, a pool, a 78 space lot, plus five hand placed fixtures in all 47 rooms, plus an L-shaped bathroom alcove retrofitted into every bespoke room, plus three light switches each. That is 235 authored fixtures on top of a full exterior for one developer, and the design says so itself. Also the office key basket makes the critical path 47 return trips across an 82m perimeter, which is the first thing that gets cut in week six.
- *THE NIGHT CATALOGUE*: It is a deduction game whose answer key does not exist. Shelf, charge and kin for 47 films means authoring 47 charges against 21 laws that carry no film citations in the data, plus balancing a three-page simultaneous lock with no per-field feedback, which is the hardest thing in Obra Dinn and took Lucas Pope years. On top of that: five levels, seven physically differentiated wings, a self-building shelf hall, a study, a basement and an unbuilt wing before the game starts. This is the three years in a trench coat.
- *THE ASSAY*: The law-diagnosis map. Forty-seven films times several plausible error directions each, mapped onto 21 laws, is a couple of hundred authored judgements that produce no mesh, no texture and no screenshot, and done lazily the whole mechanic reads as random. The design admits this is the most expensive invisible thing in it. Compounding: the laws in the shipped data have no cited evidence, so that mapping starts from zero, and a six storey ramped building has to exist before the mechanic can be tested.
- *GHOST LIGHT*: Clean versus slurred doubles the authoring of every beat in the game, so 47 numbers times three to five beats times two good-looking states is roughly 400 authored sequences, and it introduces the only timing input in the batch into a brief whose loudest requirement is inclusivity. FLY hands the camera to the room for up to six seconds, which is a motion sickness and consent problem on a public site. It also dissolves the hub, so the ledger wall stops being home.

**What every design missed.** All six designs build their progression, their ending, or both on data that the build pipeline does not emit, and in one case on data that does not exist anywhere. I measured this against public/vault-data.json as it ships and against scripts/emit_vault_data.py.

The file contains exactly ten top level keys: generated_from, queue, lessons, count, avg, films, links, shoebox, drawer, quotes. That is it.

1. THE LESSONS HAVE NO CITED EVIDENCE. The 21 taste laws are emitted as objects of exactly {rule, weight}, passed through verbatim from lessons.json. There is no film linkage of any kind, and only 1 of the 21 rules even names a film in its prose. Five of the six designs make 'a law crystallises when you have visited the films it cites' a core unlock: the Study plaques, the house rules in the laundry, the law cards in the office, the brass rods in the plant room. That join table does not exist, cannot be derived from what is there, and is 21 hand-authored evidence sets written by the one person who knows what the evidence is. It is the largest unbudgeted item in the entire batch and every design that leans on it treats it as already solved.

2. THERE IS NO PITCHED ARRAY. Zero of the 78 pitched titles are in the build output. The lobby cases, the office key rack, the parking lot stencils, the Unbuilt Wing corridor, the promotable cars: four designs build a whole zone out of content that is not in the file.

3. THERE IS NO RECOMMENDATIONS ARRAY. All 154 are absent. Every single one of the six designs ends by handing the visitor exactly one recommendation with his real reasoning on it. As shipped, not one of these six endings can be built.

4. THERE IS NO EMOTIONAL_KEY. Zero of 47 films carry it. The Night Catalogue's entire seven wing taxonomy, which is the thing that makes its building non-spoiling, is built on a field that is not in the data. It flags this as a one line fix on the last line of its document, which is the right instinct arriving in the wrong place.

5. LINKS IS 35, NOT 39. The emit script drops unresolved title-to-slug matches silently and prints a count. Four bloodlines are being lost at build time right now. Every design that makes bloodlines into physical doors is planning against a number that is 11 percent too high, and more importantly the drop is silent, so nobody noticed.

6. NO SESSION NOTES, NO MAILBOX, NO NIGHT DEBT. The 51 session notes, 5 mailbox notes and 9 night debt items are all referenced as world dressing (the office ledger book, the mail slots, the scratches inside the ice machine lid) and none of them are emitted.

Not one of the six 'first thing to build' sections is the emit pipeline. Every one of them starts with a room or a mechanic. Whichever design wins, week one is Python and Supabase: grow emit_vault_data.py to carry pitched, recommendations, emotional_key, session notes and a lesson_evidence join, and make the dropped-link count a build failure instead of a print statement. That is roughly a week, it is the least glamorous work in the project, and every design's ending is unbuildable until it is done.

The same lens catches a second thing nobody costed. dist ships ONE eagerly loaded 1.5MB App chunk that contains all 16 bespoke rooms and every one of their texture generators, plus a 147KB index chunk. Scaled to 47 bespoke rooms at the current architecture that is roughly 4MB of JavaScript before a single new mechanic is added, on top of 7.8MB of posters already in public. The recon's max-three-rooms-resident rule is a runtime budget and says nothing about download. Not one design proposes per-room lazy imports or route level code splitting, and on GitHub Pages with no server there is nothing else that saves you. Room components need to become dynamic imports keyed off the registry before room seventeen exists, not after room forty.

**Required grafts.**

- EAR TO THE DOOR, from NO VACANCY, as the highest priority graft. One BiquadFilter lowpass at ~380Hz with gain 0.15 over the audio recipe each film already has, plus a six inch camera ease toward the wood. It costs almost nothing, it fixes Second Watch's worst structural hole (37 dark doors a night are currently dead geometry) by making every one of the 47 listenable whether or not it is lit tonight, and it is simultaneously the content warning system. A player who does not want to walk into Hereditary can get the true temperature of all 47 films from the walkway and that is a complete visit.
- ROOM NUMBER EQUALS SCORE, from NO VACANCY. Second Watch throws the numbers off the world entirely and leaves them only on the Polaroid wall, which under-serves rule 8. Put the score on the door plate as a room number, generated from one canvas atlas. Zero HUD, zero leaderboard, sacred numbers rendered as the most ordinary object in a motel. Memento is Room 100 and it should be at the end of the strip.
- THE HOUSE LIGHTS SWITCH, from THE NIGHT AUDIT, as the manual trigger for Second Watch's flip. The positional reveal stays as the intended discovery, but a physical switch by the door in the same place in every room gives the same two-state transform a discoverable, repeatable, reversible handle. It makes the flip re-readable in both directions, it removes the failure mode where a player never finds the sightline and gets the silent 90 second assist instead, and it is the same code path. Take the Night Audit's rule that two of a room's fragments are legible in one state only, so the switch is load bearing rather than a toggle.
- THE BLANK NIGHTS AND THE SHARED-NIGHT CLUSTERS, from THE NIGHT AUDIT. Generated for free from the watched field already in vault-data.json. Fifteen dark units on the strip for the nights he watched nothing, and adjoining doors under one porch light for the triple features (10A The Nice Guys, 10B Batman Begins, 10C The Dark Knight, one Friday in July). This is the single strongest emotional beat anybody wrote and it costs one instanced wall and a spacing rule. It also gives the flat strip the rhythm it otherwise lacks.
- THE VAULT NEVER EDITORIALISES A NIGHT, from THE NIGHT AUDIT, verbatim, written into the repo as a hard rule beside the existing hard rules rather than left as taste. The Departed room gets a hotel pen and a stack of paper turned face down and squared to the table edge, and nothing else. The place remembers, it does not comment. This is the line that keeps the project from becoming a grief game and it is the most Dixon-respecting sentence in all six documents.
- FILL THE PAGE FROM OUTSIDE THE ROOM, from THE NIGHT CATALOGUE, translated to the strip. Everything a room gives you must also be obtainable from the walkway: the door plate, the ear, the turned Polaroid, the exit lines painted on the doors. Declining Hereditary, Se7en, Barbarian or Sicario must cost literally nothing, and it must be a real path rather than a mercy. Combined with Ear to the Door this makes the entire game playable at arm's length, which is the strongest possible answer to 'more inclusive'.
- THE CUE BUS, from GHOST LIGHT. Take the architecture, not the timing input. CurtainReveal, ScheduledCut and PulseBeat currently run off useFrame wall clocks, verified in src/rooms/systems, and that is the actual technical reason rooms read as dioramas. Re-point every system at an event the room fires, following the walkBus / gradeBus / developBus pattern the repo already uses. Second Watch's flip becomes the first cue on that bus, and every future mechanic gets it free.
- THE SECOND RACK, from THE ASSAY. Four films the doctrine cannot price: Masters of the Universe 8.5, Stardust 9.0, Rogue One 8.2, Catch Me If You Can 9.3. On the strip they are four doors whose rooms refuse to explain themselves and the game says so out loud. Any design that models a person's taste needs a visible place where it admits the model does not cover him, or the whole thing reads as a cage.
- THE ENDING VOICE, from THE ASSAY, over Second Watch's exit reading. Describe the visitor, do not score them, and be allowed to be rude. Specifically steal applying one of his own laws back to the player's route ('do not split this one across two nights'), which is the moment the building reveals it was reading you. Keep Second Watch's divergence-not-agreement rule, which is the better selection criterion.
- THE DOOR PLATE IN CALLBOARD REGISTER, from GHOST LIGHT. The plate already carries date, platform and rewatch. Add the house note voice for anything flashPolicy.js touches, naming which moment it happens in, so the warning is specific and diegetic rather than a modal. flashPolicy.js already exists and should govern the flip in both directions.

**Winner: NO VACANCY.** It is the only design where the score stops being data without stopping being visible. A room number is the one place in the world where a number on a wall is not a UI element, and the door density readable from the courtyard (five doors under 7.0, then 53 metres of blank stucco, then 21 doors packed into the nines with five frames touching at 9.6) teaches a stranger this man's entire taste in three seconds in no language at all. I checked those counts against public/vault-data.json and they are exact, which tells me this design read the data rather than gesturing at it. It also got the link count right at 35 when four of the other five said 39.

On my lens it wins on verbs. Everyone else's interaction budget collapses to Walk plus one thing: NIGHT AUDIT is Walk plus a binary switch, SECOND WATCH is Walk plus one secret, GHOST LIGHT is Walk plus a timing release, CATALOGUE and ASSAY are Walk plus a menu you fill in. NO VACANCY has Listen, Open, Close, Take, Hang, Switch across three separate lamps plus six breakers, and Sit, and every one of them delivers content rather than acknowledging it. Three switches per room means one room is three rooms, which is more state than the binary house lights and it is the direct fix for the measured rot that 16 of 29 shipped interactions are nudge.

It also contains the runner-up. NIGHT AUDIT's best structural idea is that his chronology is the truthful order, and NO VACANCY already ships it: the office wire basket always holds exactly one key and it is always the next film in his real watch order, so the building is score-spatial and the progression is chronological at the same time. NIGHT AUDIT can only have one axis. NO VACANCY has three (chronology from the office, affinity from the closets, quality from the building) and lets the player switch between them at any door.

And it is the only one that answers rule 9 with architecture instead of a framing device. The guest is a guest, Room 4 is his and everything else is a guest room, so a stranger is never made to be an intruder in a diary or a hired investigator solving a man. Ear to the Door means the entire 47-door building is playable at arm's length by someone who never opens anything, and that is a complete visit rather than a lesser one. It is not the cheapest design here and I am not pretending otherwise; NIGHT AUDIT is genuinely cheaper and SECOND WATCH is cheapest by a distance. But the fixture kit is built once at 13 draw calls and reused 47 times, it pays for itself by deleting InfoSurfaces, and the vertical slice has a real kill criterion answerable in two weeks: does Hereditary at 6.7 carry three minutes on the bare armature with no bespoke world at all. If it does, all 47 carry and the 16 bespoke rooms become gravy instead of the entire budget.

**Fatal flaws called:**

- *NO VACANCY*: 235 hand-placed fixtures plus a four-storey exterior is the largest confirmed authoring load of any design that isn't also asking for 47 new authored sequences, and the five-fixture armature will fight BR2049's greenhouse and Sicario's tunnel hard enough to make two good rooms worse.
- *THE NIGHT AUDIT*: Read is passive and Hang and Ring fire once each, so the actual verb set is Walk plus a binary light switch, and by room five the player flicks it reflexively on entry and the revelation has become a routine with 42 rooms still to go.
- *THE SECOND WATCH*: One secret per room means the room is finished the moment it is found, so ten doors times ninety seconds is the entire game, and throttling a stranger to 10 of 47 films is a content cap dressed as curation in a proposal that was asked for MORE room to explore.
- *THE NIGHT CATALOGUE*: Obra Dinn works because the fate is physically depicted in the tableau; here the answer is a stranger's arbitrary decimal and which of 21 unwritten laws he privately applied, which is not in the room and cannot be put there, so the three-page lock becomes guess-and-recombine and the loop dies in twenty minutes.
- *GHOST LIGHT*: The proscenium is a default costume that overwrites every film's world with the same theatre, which is the exact opposite of more themed to each movie, and it demotes the one genuinely good built thing in the repo, the motel, into set dressing for a single room.
- *THE ASSAY*: The building leaks the score band from the street and then asks you to guess the tenth, but 30 of 47 films sit between 7.4 and 9.9 and the tenth is not derivable from anything, so the core act is guessing a stranger's arbitrary decimal and a player learns that it is unguessable after about five misses.

**What every design missed.** Every single one of these opens in the dark and asks for a walk before it gives anything, and not one of them designs the first twenty seconds on a phone. This ships as a public GitHub Pages link, which means first contact is overwhelmingly a stranger holding a 390px screen with one thumb, no pointer lock, no keyboard, no context, and roughly twenty seconds of patience. The six cold opens are: a wet street at night, a dark parking lot, a night manager's office, a seat facing a bare bulb, a dark motel walkway, and a concrete strip under a sodium lamp. Every one of them takes away the single thing the current build does best, which is putting a wall of 47 Polaroids in front of a stranger in one glance with zero input, and replaces it with darkness and a request for locomotion. Touch appears in all six as an accessibility bullet ("touch stick", "or tap") rather than as the primary platform the whole thing is designed against, and no design specifies what the first screen looks like at 390px, what happens with no pointer lock, or what a visitor who never once moves is given. The winner needs a designed arrival: something that is legible and moving in the first three seconds on a phone with no input at all, that then invites the walk. NO VACANCY is closest because its courtyard read (blank ground floor, packed fourth floor) is a single glance that teaches the whole archive, but it currently happens after you leave Room 4 and turn a corner, which is already fifteen seconds and two inputs too late.

Two verified facts nobody budgeted for, since four of six designs gate their progression on them. First, the taste laws do not cite evidence. They ship as {rule, weight} and exactly 1 of 21 names a film in its text; the entire "a law crystallises when you have visited the films it cites" mechanic rests on a 21-by-N citation map that does not exist and that somebody has to hand-author with taste. Second, public/vault-data.json contains no emotional_key and no pitched titles at all, so NIGHT CATALOGUE's seven wings and every design's treatment of the 78 pitched need emit_vault_data.py changes before a single wall gets modelled. Also links is 35, not 39, and src/rooms/families/ holds one file, so THE ASSAY's plan to auto-generate 78 rooms from a "six-family template engine" is generating them from a template engine that has one family in it.

**Required grafts.**

- From THE NIGHT AUDIT, Layer 4 whole: the context line becomes physical evidence and never text. NO VACANCY's five fixtures cover the take, the verdict, the plot and the bloodlines but not the NIGHT. Barbarian gets one wine glass, one ring, and the DO NOT DISTURB card on the wrong side of the door. Annihilation gets shoes still by the door and the TV as the only light because he was wiped after six hours of rehearsal. Rogue One has no context line so it gets a made bed, because absence is information.
- From THE NIGHT AUDIT, the hard rule that governs it: the Vault never editorialises a night. Room 99A is The Departed and the divorce papers were signed that day, and what is in that room is a hotel pen and a stack of paper turned face down and squared to the table edge, and the Vault says nothing. Make this a written rule in the room-authoring spec, not a preference, or the whole building drifts into a grief game.
- From THE SECOND WATCH, score as attention rather than construction. Every room stays fully built and fully dressed at every score; what changes is whether it notices you. 9.5+ withholds and waits, 7.x runs a loop that ignores you, under 7.0 talks over you. Disclosure Day at 5.2 keeps the biggest room in the building and gives the speech whether you came in or not. Free, funny, honest, and it stops room 52 from reading as a punishment box.
- From THE SECOND WATCH, one sightline per room that only resolves from one position on the floor. NO VACANCY's L-shaped bathroom alcove kills the doorway problem but gives every room the same shape of secret. Add one per-room positional reveal on top of it, built as a second grade plus light preset over identical geometry, which is the same invertible-transform shape fadedConfigFor and developedGradeFor already prove works in this codebase. The Sting's plywood back wall and Coherence's infinite window are already fully specified and should be built as written.
- From THE SECOND WATCH, the 90-second silent auto-reveal and the two-second proximity dwell fallback on every touch target. Assistance that announces itself is not assistance, and the dwell fallback means nobody is ever locked out of content by their aim. DwellConcede already ships.
- From THE SECOND WATCH, two doors out of every room, each painted with a real sentence he said about that film, and you leave by whichever is closer to what you felt. NO VACANCY's clerk currently reads only dwell and route, which measures attention but not disagreement, and divergence is the only signal that makes an exit reading about the visitor instead of about him.
- From GHOST LIGHT, SIT as a first-class verb rather than the TV trigger it currently is. Sitting on the bed should let the room perform itself hands-off at full quality, with any input standing you back up. That makes the whole motel completable with one thumb on a phone, which is the platform this thing actually ships to, and it costs an autopilot walking an existing path.
- From THE ASSAY, the decree as a visible event. NO VACANCY already numbers Sicario and The Departed as 99 and 99A with no wall between the frames. When the player first opens 99A, the plate on Room 98's Matrix door, in sightline down the same fourth-floor walkway, physically slides from 99 to 98 and settles, because that is what he did. Nobody else in the set built the one moment a stranger would describe to a friend.
- From THE ASSAY, the config-schema rule that one prop per room is a mechanism and not decor, and the ban on nudge as a default. If a touchable does not change a state that is part of the room's argument, it does not get a prompt. This is the direct fix for 16 of 29 interactions being nudge and it is a schema constraint, not a content pass.
- From THE NIGHT CATALOGUE, the guarantee that everything a room gives you is also obtainable from outside it. NO VACANCY has the dawn drawer holding every key, which fixes lockout, but a player who never wants to be inside Hereditary should be able to get its take from the walkway via Ear to the Door and the door card. Make that a hard authoring rule for the dread rooms specifically.
- From THE NIGHT CATALOGUE, the Dark Drawer room: a chair, a light, and a card that reads what he remembers, which is nothing. NO VACANCY buries the 30 unscorable titles in basement boxes; four draw calls buys them the most honest room in the building instead.
- From THE NIGHT AUDIT, the register as the exit artifact. NO VACANCY's clerk hands over a recommendation card and a list of room numbers; the shift-report voice ("went straight to 11B and stayed nine minutes, which is longer than the movie deserved") is a far better souvenir than a list, and it is the same data rendered with a personality.
